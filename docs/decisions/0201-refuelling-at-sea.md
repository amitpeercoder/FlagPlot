# 0201: Refuelling at sea

Status: a plan by the architect, written on Nov 21, 2026 for the designer's queue of rules. It takes up what decisions 0045, 0046 and 0112 deferred. It was read by a reviewer, and then rewritten on research the designer asked for (`docs/research/fuelling-at-sea.md`), which overturned two things the first plan assumed: that forces stop to fuel, and that they fuel by day only. This is the plan as rewritten; it goes to a reviewer again before its hand calculation is written.

Summary: A ship takes fuel only in a port of her side. A force with no port in reach goes home or runs dry, and the oilers that kept the carriers at sea in 1942 are left out of every scenario because they could do nothing. This gives a ship that can fuel others at sea a mark on her class, and an order by which a force fuels from her force while both steam on together at a slow speed, by day or by night, a few ships at a time. A force attacked while fuelling takes no fuel that pulse. The staff counts an oiler's force as a place to fuel.

Standing: Planned on Nov 21, 2026, rewritten on the research; waits for its second review.

## Why

What is so today:

- **Fuel is taken in port and nowhere else.** A force stopped at the centre of a hex holding a base of its side fuels with no order, in each pulse's loading work (decisions 0045 and 0112). The rulebook says "there is no refuelling at sea (no oiler, no transfer between ships)".
- **A ship can carry fuel as cargo,** a separate figure from the fuel she steams on, loaded and landed at a base.
- **There is no oiler.** The Coral Sea's Neosho and Midway's Cimarron and Platte are left out of their scenarios because "the game refuels only in port, so Neosho fuels nobody at sea" (decision 0188, 47).
- **Two forces of a side can lie in one hex,** and an escort keeps station in its charge's hex and takes its route; nothing passes between forces at sea.
- **A force with no home port has its fuel weighed against nothing,** and two agent commanders ran destroyers nearly dry at the Coral Sea with no warning.

What the record says (the research, 90 rows):

- **Ships fuelled under way, in every case found from 1941 to 1945,** in both navies. Destroyers had fuelled under way since 1917 and carriers since 1939. No case of ships stopped in open sea was found.
- **The speed:** the Pacific Fleet's instructions of 28 August 1942 set 8 knots as standard, 12 at most for the fast oilers, 8 at most in heavy weather, the sea two points on the bow, and no zigzag with heavy ships alongside. Later in the war 10 to 12 knots.
- **By night as well as by day,** when an operation needed it: Enterprise on 28 January 1942, the Coral Sea forces of both sides in May, the Japanese supply forces as a routine in the autumn of 1942. The one rule against it found is American and of 1945.
- **The rate:** the instructions plan for 1,500 to 2,000 barrels an hour a hose (227 to 303 tons), a large ship taking two hoses. Japanese cruisers and battleships in 1942 took mostly 200 to 310 tons an hour.
- **Alongside:** destroyers on both sides of an oiler at once; a large ship on one side only.
- **Under threat:** the doctrine is to fuel fast and be able to cast off at once. Task Force 17 broke off on a radar contact on 6 May 1942 and resumed.
- **Weather** stopped or slowed it: four destroyers in ten hours at the Wake relief.
- **Warships fuelled their own destroyers,** commonly, from 1942.

## The rules

### The ship that gives

1. **A ship's class may say that she fuels others at sea.** It is a mark on the class, beside its room for cargo, not a new kind of ship: an oiler is an auxiliary with the mark. A class file may leave the column out, and then no ship has it.
2. **What she gives is the fuel she carries as cargo.** The fuel she steams on is her own and is never given. She loads cargo fuel at a base by the orders for cargo, as any ship does.
3. **A ship with the mark takes nothing at sea.** Oilers do not fuel each other.

### The order

4. **A force is given the order "fuel from" another force of its side.** It is refused where that force has no ship with the mark, or is the force itself.
5. **The force goes to the giving force and keeps with it,** as an escort keeps with its charge: it steers for the giver's hex, and once there takes the giver's route. The giver's own orders are not changed by it; where she goes is her commander's.
6. **The order ends of itself** when every ship of the force is full, or the giver holds no cargo fuel, or the giver is sunk or leaves the map. The force is then where it is, with no route, to be ordered again. A new route or any order to move ends it too.

### Fuelling

7. **Fuel passes in a pulse at whose start the two forces are in one hex,** the force is under the order, a ship of it lacks fuel and the giver holds cargo fuel. It passes for the hours of the pulse, by day or by night.
8. **Both forces steam slowly while it passes.** In such a pulse neither makes more than the fuelling speed, 8 knots, on the giver's route: 48 nm in a six-hour pulse and 96 nm in the night's twelve. A giver with no route lies where she is and so does the force.
9. **A force attacked takes nothing.** In a pulse in which either force is attacked from the air or fights a surface action, no fuel passes in that pulse, and neither force is slowed by this rule in it: they have cast off.
10. **An oiler has two lines.** A destroyer takes one. A larger ship takes one, and only one of the two lines may serve a larger ship at a time: so two destroyers at once, or a larger ship and a destroyer. Each line serves ships in turn through the pulse's hours; a ship uses the hours her tons take at the rate, and the next takes what is left of the line.
11. **Ships are taken in a fixed order:** a ship out of fuel first, then the ship with the smallest share of her capacity aboard, then by id. A ship takes from one oiler in a pulse; with two oilers in the giving force, she goes to the one of lower id that has hours left.
12. **Each ship takes the least of** what she lacks, the cargo fuel the oiler still holds, and the rate times the hours of her line that are left.
13. **A force out of fuel can be fuelled where the giver comes to it.** A force that has run dry lies where it stopped; under the order, a giver that steams into its hex fuels it by rules 7 to 12, and the first ship fuelled sets the force going again. This is new: in port, and in the first plan, a force adrift between two hexes could not be fuelled.
14. **A port is used before an oiler.** A ship fuelled by a base in a pulse takes nothing from an oiler in it, and an oiler that did cargo work in a pulse gives nothing in it.

### What is recorded and who knows

15. **An event for each ship fuelled at sea,** naming the ship, the oiler and the tons, with a breakdown of what she lacked, what the oiler held, the hours and the rate; its cause is the order. An event when fuelling is broken off by an attack, citing the attack. An event when the order ends, saying why. Each side sees its own and nothing of the enemy's. They are placed in the list of who sees what, the briefing, the agent's tools, the export, the viewer and the table.
16. **No new state but the order.** The fuel given leaves the oiler's cargo and joins the ship's own; both figures exist and are hashed today. The order is a force's order like its others, and is hashed and saved with them.

### The staff

17. **A warning where nothing can be done.** A force with no home port, whose ship is below a quarter of her class's fuel capacity, is named in the staff's report each day. Nothing else changes for it. This is all that applies where a side has no oiler, so no game on file plays differently.
18. **An oiler's force is a place to fuel.** Where a side has a force with an oiler holding cargo fuel, a force of the staff's that is short of fuel for its task, and has no base nearer by sea than the oiler, is given the order to fuel from her; when the order ends it goes back to its task. The staff reads the oiler's cargo fuel and her class's mark from its own side's view.
19. **The staff places no oiler.** She is put where she steams by her commander's route. An order for where oilers wait, and a doctrine that sends one to reload, are later decisions.

## Figures

20. **The fuelling speed is 8 knots,** the standard of the instructions of August 1942, for both navies. The Japanese record gives one speed, 14 knots on one day, and no rule; 8 is used for both and said to be the American figure.
21. **The rate of a line is 250 tons an hour,** inside the 227 to 303 the instructions plan for and the 200 to 310 the Japanese rows show. It is a figure of the game sized from those rows, the same for every oiler.
22. **Two lines, and one larger ship at a time,** from the instructions' "destroyers on both sides, large ships on the port side only".
23. **The oilers' cargo of oil** is read from a statement of the time: 12,000 tons each loaded in Platte and Cimarron in July 1942, to be entered as rows.
24. **A quarter of capacity,** the staff's warning, is a figure of the doctrine.

## Stages

25. **Stage A is the rule,** on placeholder classes, with its scenario and hand calculation.
26. **Stage B makes real oilers.** The derivation gains a ship type for an oiler, a class's room for cargo read from rows (it is written as nothing today), and the mark; and the rows are gathered that the Cimarron class lacks, her own bunkers and her endurance at a speed.
27. **Stage C is warships fuelling their own destroyers,** which the record shows was common. It needs a rule for how far a warship draws down her own fuel, and no rate for it was found; it is planned when A and B are played.

28. **Stage D is the staff planning fuel, on the designer's word of Nov 21, 2026.** Rules 18 and 19 are the least the staff does and are not where it ends. A staff plans a force's fuel as it plans its supply: it does not wait for a ship to run low. Stage D is a decision of its own, written when stage A is built, and is to give:
    - **a forecast for every force the staff has tasked:** the day each ship reaches the margin of its risk on the movement the plan asks, the destroyers first since they have the shortest legs;
    - **a fuelling planned before that day,** at a base or from an oiler, whichever the plan can reach, at a place outside the circles of danger of decision 0202;
    - **oilers as means the staff may take,** as it takes free carriers: one sent with a force that has far to go, or to a meeting on its way, and sent to reload when she is empty;
    - **the fuelling shown in the plan and in its dry run,** as a phase with its day and place, and what the plan cannot do without it;
    - **long passages planned with their fuelling,** which meets decision 0203, the way a force takes.

## Rule card

Reads: a class's mark, room for cargo and fuel capacity; a ship's fuel and cargo fuel; a force's order, place and route; whether a force was attacked in the pulse; the three figures. Writes: a ship's fuel, an oiler's cargo fuel, the distance two forces make in a pulse, a force's state when it is set going or its order ends. Draws no random number.

## What it does not do

- **No weather.** The game has none, so the swell that held the Wake relief to four destroyers in ten hours cannot be shown.
- **No course into the sea, and no loss of the zigzag.** The forces fuel on whatever course the giver steers.
- **No submarines,** so the threat that made commanders fuel fast and far from the enemy is only what aircraft and surface ships can do.
- No aviation fuel or other stores passed at sea.
- No oiler is added to a scenario of record by this decision. The Coral Sea and Midway are looked at again when stage B is built.

## What it costs

- **A new order and a new way for two forces to move together,** in the part of the engine that moves ships. The second review reads it against that code before anything is built.
- **Rulebook:** chapter 2 (fuelling, the order, and the two sentences that say there is none at sea), chapter 8 (the staff's place to fuel and its warning), the glossary ("oiler", "fuelling at sea", "line"), and the correction of chapter 2's sentence that a route order sets a force out of fuel going, which the code and the glossary deny.
- **Versions:** the state's layout rises with the new order. The rules label rises only if a game on file plays differently, and none should: no class on file has the mark.

## The designer's word, Nov 21, 2026

He held that fuelling at night was done when operations required it, and that ships did not lie stopped to fuel once submarines made that deadly, and asked for research before the rule was settled. The research bears him out on the night in full. On stopping, it found that ships fuelled under way throughout this war and before it, so the rule is slow steaming from the first day of every scenario.

## Amended on the second review

A reviewer who had not seen the rewritten plan read it against the engine's movement code, the staff and the research on Nov 21, 2026. Where this and the text above differ, this holds. The rules are folded into one statement before the hand calculation is written.

**The order is its own, not an escort's (replaces rules 5 and 6 where they differ).**

29. **A force under the order holds the giver's force in a field of its own.** It is not made an escort or a charge: those words bring the escort's place in a raid and its guns, which are not meant here. Only the escort's way of steering for a force and taking its route is reused.
30. **It is refused where the forces are tied to others:** the giver is herself under the order, is an escort or has an escort; the force is the giver for another, is an escort or has an escort. So no chain of forces each following the next can form. Fuelling with escorts in company waits for a later decision.
31. **The order also ends when the giver has no ship with the mark afloat in her force,** as when she is detached, joined to another force or sent to the yard. When it ends the force is at cruise with no route.

**When in the pulse (replaces rules 7 to 9).**

32. **Which forces fuel is settled when the ships move,** at the start of that step: the two forces are in one hex, the force is under the order, a ship of it lacks fuel and the giver holds cargo fuel.
33. **Both then make no more than the fuelling speed for the whole pulse,** on the giver's route, whether or not the fuelling is broken off. The slow speed is the speed the force reports, so anything that reads a force's speed reads it.
34. **The fuel passes at the end of that step,** when every fight of the pulse is known. It does not pass if a raid attacked a ship of either force in the pulse, or either force fought a surface action in it.

**A force that has run dry (replaces rule 13).**

35. **A force out of fuel is fuelled only in a pulse in which the giver lies still in its hex,** with no route or halted there. An oiler steaming through at any speed leaves a ship that cannot move behind. The first ship fuelled sets the force idle where it is, its route dropped, as in port.
36. **The staff cannot rescue such a force,** since it places no oiler; that is the commander's until stage D.

**The lines and the rate (replaces rules 10 to 12, 21 and 22).**

37. **An oiler has two lines of 250 tons an hour each.** A destroyer takes one. Any other ship takes both, as the instructions give a large ship two hoses: 500 tons an hour, against the 465 Enterprise took on 8 June 1942 and the 400 of 1944. It is more than the Japanese cruisers of 1942 took, 200 to 310, and that is said.
38. **Each ship is an hour alongside before her fuel passes,** for the approach and the rig. It is a figure of the game, sized from the days on record: a cruiser and two destroyers in three and a half hours, five ships in fifteen and a half. Without it two lines would fill ten destroyers in a pulse, which no day on record shows.
39. **Ships are taken in one order across the force:** the smallest share of capacity aboard first, then by id. A destroyer starts on a line when one is free; another ship when both are. A ship whose hour and fuel do not fit in what is left of the pulse takes what fits and is first in the next.

**State (replaces rule 16).**

40. **The order is new state:** a field of a task force, hashed in its place and named, with the state's layout raised and the golden files remade. What is worked within a pulse, which pairs fuel and the hours used, is kept only for the pulse.

**The staff (replaces rule 18 where it differs).**

41. **"Short" is the doctrine's own measure.** For the carrier doctrine and the stand doctrine: a force that would be sent home for fuel today is sent to an oiler instead where the passage to her present place, as the command's picture has it, is less than the passage home; and a force with no home port is sent to her when a ship is below a quarter. While the order stands the staff gives the force no route, as it gives none while a force fuels in port.
42. **The warning of rule 17 may change the staff's words in games on file.** The build plays every scenario and recorded game before and after and says which words moved; no game's events or hashes may.

**Figures (replaces rules 20 and 23).**

43. **8 knots is the American standard for the approach; the instructions allow 12 for the fast oilers.** For the Japanese side it is a placeholder: their record gives "slow speed" and, once, 14 knots.
44. **An oiler's cargo of oil is settled in stage B,** by a rule across rows that disagree: a load of 12,000 tons in July 1942, and capacities of 10,317, 13,226 and about 15,900 tons. Stage A's oilers are placeholders.

**Stage B also needs,** beyond what rule 26 names: a ship type the derivation can map to an auxiliary; an attribute for cargo oil; and the loader accepting a class file with no column for the mark.
