# 0201: Refuelling at sea

Status: a plan by the architect, written on Nov 21, 2026 for the designer's queue of rules. It takes up what decisions 0045, 0046 and 0112 deferred. It was read by a reviewer, rewritten on research the designer asked for (`docs/research/fuelling-at-sea.md`), and read again. This is the plan as it stands after both; its hand calculation, scenario 115, is written from this text.

Summary: A ship takes fuel only in a port of her side. A force with no port in reach goes home or runs dry, and the oilers that kept the carriers at sea in 1942 are left out of every scenario because they could do nothing. This gives a ship that can fuel others at sea a mark on her class, and an order by which a force fuels from her force while both steam on together at a slow speed, by day or by night, a few ships at a time. A force attacked while fuelling takes no fuel that pulse. The staff warns of a force running low and sends it to an oiler where one is nearer than home.

Standing: Planned on Nov 21, 2026, rewritten on the research and amended on two reviews; waits for its build. Stage A is this decision's build; stages B, C and D follow.

## Why

What is so today:

- **Fuel is taken in port and nowhere else.** A force stopped at the centre of a hex holding a base of its side fuels with no order, in each pulse's loading work (decisions 0045 and 0112). The rulebook says "there is no refuelling at sea (no oiler, no transfer between ships)".
- **A ship can carry fuel as cargo,** a separate figure from the fuel she steams on, loaded and landed at a base.
- **There is no oiler.** The Coral Sea's Neosho and Midway's Cimarron and Platte are left out of their scenarios because "the game refuels only in port, so Neosho fuels nobody at sea" (decision 0188, 47).
- **An escort keeps station in its charge's hex and takes its route;** nothing passes between forces at sea.
- **A force with no home port has its fuel weighed against nothing,** and two agent commanders ran destroyers nearly dry at the Coral Sea with no warning.

What the record says (the research, 90 rows):

- **Ships fuelled under way, in every case found from 1941 to 1945,** in both navies. Destroyers had fuelled under way since 1917 and carriers since 1939. No case of ships stopped in open sea was found.
- **The speed:** the Pacific Fleet's instructions of 28 August 1942 set 8 knots as standard for the approach and allow 12 for the fast oilers. Later in the war 10 to 12 knots. The Japanese record gives "slow speed" and, once, 14 knots.
- **By night as well as by day,** when an operation needed it: Enterprise on 28 January 1942, the Coral Sea forces of both sides in May, the Japanese supply forces as a routine in the autumn of 1942. The one rule against it found is American and of 1945.
- **The rate:** the instructions plan for 1,500 to 2,000 barrels an hour a hose (227 to 303 tons), a large ship taking two hoses. Enterprise took about 465 tons an hour on 8 June 1942; Japanese cruisers and battleships in 1942 mostly 200 to 310.
- **How many:** a cruiser and two destroyers in three and a half hours; five ships in fifteen and a half; four destroyers in ten hours in a swell.
- **Under threat:** fuel fast and be able to cast off at once. Task Force 17 broke off on a radar contact on 6 May 1942 and resumed.
- **Warships fuelled their own destroyers,** commonly, from 1942.

## The rules

### The ship that gives

1. **A ship's class may say that she fuels others at sea.** It is a mark on the class, beside its room for cargo, not a new kind of ship: an oiler is an auxiliary with the mark. A class file may leave the column out, and then no ship has it.
2. **What she gives is the fuel she carries as cargo.** The fuel she steams on is her own and is never given. She loads cargo fuel at a base by the orders for cargo, as any ship does.
3. **A ship with the mark takes nothing at sea.** Oilers do not fuel each other.

### The order

4. **A force is given the order "fuel from" another force of its side,** the giver. The order is a field of the force, its own: the force is not made an escort or a charge.
5. **It is refused** where the giver has no ship with the mark; is the force itself; is herself under the order, is an escort or has an escort; or where the force is the giver for another, is an escort or has an escort. So no chain of forces each following the next can form.
6. **The force steers for the giver's hex, and once there takes the giver's route,** by the escort's way of doing both and nothing else of the escort's. The giver's own orders are not changed; where she goes is her commander's.
7. **The order ends of itself** when every ship of the force is full; when the giver holds no cargo fuel; when the giver has no ship with the mark afloat in her force, as when she is sunk, detached, joined to another force or sent to the yard; or when the giver leaves the map. A new route or any order to move ends it too. When it ends the force is at cruise with no route.

### Fuelling

8. **Which forces fuel is settled when the ships move,** at the start of that step of the pulse: the two forces are in one hex, the force is under the order, a ship of it lacks fuel and the giver holds cargo fuel.
9. **Both then make no more than the fuelling speed for the whole pulse,** on the giver's route. It is the speed the force reports, so whatever reads a force's speed reads it. A giver with no route lies where she is and so does the force.
10. **The fuel passes at the end of that step,** when every fight of the pulse is known, by day or by night. It does not pass if a raid attacked a ship of either force in the pulse, or either force fought a surface action in it. The forces are slow for the pulse all the same.
11. **An oiler has two lines.** A destroyer takes one; any other ship takes both.
12. **Each ship is alongside for a time before her fuel passes,** for the approach and the rig, and then takes fuel at the rate of her lines.
13. **Ships are taken in one order across the force:** the smallest share of capacity aboard first, then by id. A destroyer starts when a line is free; another ship when both are; and no ship starts before one earlier in the order has started. A ship whose time alongside and fuel do not fit in what is left of the pulse takes what fits; what she still lacks waits for the next pulse, in which she is ordered again by this rule and is alongside again.
14. **Each ship takes the least of** what she lacks, the cargo fuel the oiler still holds, and the rate of her lines times the hours she has them. With two oilers in the giving force a ship goes to the one of lower id that still has hours and fuel, and takes from one oiler in a pulse.
15. **A force out of fuel is fuelled only in a pulse in which the giver lies still in its hex,** with no route or halted there; where the giver is under way through the hex the two are not a pair, no fuel passes and the giver is not slowed. The first ship fuelled sets the force idle where it is, its route dropped, as in port.
16. **A port is used before an oiler.** A ship fuelled by a base in a pulse takes nothing from an oiler in it, and an oiler that did cargo work in a pulse gives nothing in it.

### What is recorded and who knows

17. **An event for each ship fuelled at sea,** naming the ship, the oiler and the tons, with a breakdown of what she lacked, what the oiler held, the hours and the rate; its cause is the order. An event when fuelling is broken off by an attack, citing the attack. An event when the order ends, saying why. Each side sees its own and nothing of the enemy's. They are placed in the list of who sees what, the briefing, the agent's tools, the export, the viewer and the table.
18. **The order is new state:** a field of a task force, hashed in its place and named, with the state's layout raised and the golden files remade. What is worked within a pulse, which pairs fuel and the hours of each line used, is kept only for the pulse.

### The staff

19. **A warning where nothing can be done.** A force with no home port, whose ship is below a quarter of her class's fuel capacity, is named in the staff's report each day.
20. **An oiler's force is a place to fuel.** For the carrier doctrine and the stand doctrine: a force that would be sent home for fuel today is given the order to fuel from an oiler's force instead, where the passage to her present place, as the command's picture has it, is less than the passage home; and a force with no home port is given it when a ship is below a quarter. The oiler must hold cargo fuel. While the order stands the staff gives the force no route, as it gives none while a force fuels in port; when it ends the force goes back to its task.
21. **The staff places no oiler and cannot rescue a force that has run dry.** She is put where she steams by her commander's route. That is stage D.

## Figures

22. **The fuelling speed is 8 knots:** 48 nm in a six-hour pulse and 96 nm in the night's twelve. It is the American standard for the approach; for the Japanese side it is a placeholder.
23. **A line passes 250 tons an hour.** A ship on both takes 500, against the 465 Enterprise took and the 400 of 1944; it is more than the Japanese cruisers of 1942 took, 200 to 310, and that is said.
24. **A ship is one hour alongside before her fuel passes.** It is a figure of the game, sized from the days on record. Without it two lines would fill ten destroyers in a pulse, which no day on record shows.
25. **A quarter of capacity,** the staff's warning, is a figure of the doctrine.
26. **An oiler's cargo of oil is settled in stage B.** Stage A's oilers are placeholders.

All four figures are placeholders in the game's sense, each with what it rests on written beside it.

## Stages

27. **Stage A is the rule,** on placeholder classes, with scenario 115 and its hand calculation.
28. **Stage B makes real oilers.** The derivation gains a ship type it can map to an auxiliary, an attribute for cargo oil read from rows, and the mark; the loader accepts a class file with no column for the mark; and the rows are gathered that the Cimarron class lacks, her own bunkers and her endurance at a speed. Her cargo is settled by a rule across rows that disagree: a load of 12,000 tons in July 1942, and capacities of 10,317, 13,226 and about 15,900 tons.
29. **Stage C is warships fuelling their own destroyers,** which the record shows was common. It needs a rule for how far a warship draws down her own fuel, and no rate for it was found.
30. **Stage D is the staff planning fuel, on the designer's word of Nov 21, 2026.** A staff plans a force's fuel as it plans its supply: it does not wait for a ship to run low. Stage D is a decision of its own, written when stage A is built, and is to give:
    - **a forecast for every force the staff has tasked:** the day each ship reaches the margin of its risk on the movement the plan asks, the destroyers first;
    - **a fuelling planned before that day,** at a base or from an oiler, at a place outside the circles of danger of decision 0202;
    - **oilers as means the staff may take,** as it takes free carriers: one sent with a force that has far to go, or to a meeting on its way, and sent to reload when she is empty;
    - **the fuelling shown in the plan and in its dry run,** as a phase with its day and place, and what the plan cannot do without it;
    - **long passages planned with their fuelling,** which meets decision 0203, the way a force takes.

## Rule card

Reads: a class's mark, room for cargo and fuel capacity; a ship's fuel and cargo fuel; a force's order, place, route and speed; whether a force was attacked in the pulse; the four figures. Writes: a ship's fuel, an oiler's cargo fuel, a force's order, the speed two forces make in a pulse, a force's state when it is set going or its order ends. Draws no random number.

## Games on file

No class on file has the mark, so no game's events or hashes move, though every golden file is remade for the new layout. The warning of rule 19 may change the staff's words in games on file: the build plays every scenario and recorded game before and after and says which words moved, and stops if the record of a closed proof would.

## What it does not do

- **No weather.** The game has none, so the swell that held the Wake relief to four destroyers in ten hours cannot be shown.
- **No course into the sea, and no loss of the zigzag.** The forces fuel on whatever course the giver steers.
- **No submarines,** so the threat that made commanders fuel fast and far from the enemy is only what aircraft and surface ships can do.
- **No fuelling with escorts in company,** by rule 5.
- No aviation fuel or other stores passed at sea.
- No oiler is added to a scenario of record by this decision. The Coral Sea and Midway are looked at again when stage B is built.

## What it costs

- **A new order and a new way for two forces to move together,** in the part of the engine that moves ships.
- **Four placeholder figures** where the record gives ranges and single days.
- **Rulebook:** chapter 2 (fuelling, the order, and the two sentences that say there is none at sea), chapter 8 (the staff's place to fuel and its warning), the glossary ("oiler", "fuelling at sea", "line"), and the correction of chapter 2's sentence that a route order sets a force out of fuel going, which the code and the glossary deny.

## The designer's word, Nov 21, 2026

He held that fuelling at night was done when operations required it, and that ships did not lie stopped to fuel once submarines made that deadly, and asked for research before the rule was settled. The research bears him out on the night in full. On stopping, it found that ships fuelled under way throughout this war and before it, so the rule is slow steaming from the first day of every scenario. He then asked that the staff plan fuel and not wait for a ship to run dry, and show it in the plan: that is stage D.

## How the plan was made

Written with forces stopped and fuelling by day; read by a first reviewer; rewritten on the research (under way, by day or night, an order of its own); read by a second reviewer (the order not an escort's, the timing within a pulse, both lines for a large ship, the hour alongside, a force that has run dry). This text replaces all of them.
