# Flag Plot | The game of command

Your pilots report a carrier. The sighting is several hours old. Your own flight decks are still in action, the garrison needs its next convoy, and the staff wants to know how much you’re willing to risk. You have enough information to make a decision. You’ll have to decide whether it’s enough to make a good one.

**Flag Plot** is an operational wargame of the Pacific War. It takes its name from the compartment on a flagship where the admiral and staff plotted the battle and issued orders. If you enjoy grand strategy, the familiar problems are here at the scale of a battle or campaign. Which objective comes first? Which forces can you spare? What will today’s decision cost you next week?

## Command by intent

An **intent** describes the situation you want to bring about. *Hold Midway* tells the staff what must remain true. *Keep this base supplied* tells it what the garrison must have on hand. You choose the end and set limits, such as how much risk to accept. The staff works out the tasks and the orders needed to carry them out.

Say you want a base to hold. The staff might arrange air cover and plan a supply run if shipping and a source of supply are available. Each task sits under the intent it serves. Open **Why** on a task to see what the staff weighed, what held it back, and what could change its decision. If the plan asks for leave, you can read it and decide whether to approve it. You can also give the staff standing permission to launch eligible plans when ready.

You can take a task force, air group, or other supported unit into your own hands and give it a direct order. A held unit is out of the staff’s plans until you return it. That gives you a reason to think twice before intervening. The unit you take for one urgent job may be the unit the staff needs elsewhere.

## The war as your side knows it

The chart shows your own forces and what your side has learned about the enemy. An enemy marker may come from a sighting with the wrong ship count, type, or position. A reported hit is a crew’s claim. A quiet patch of ocean may simply be a patch nobody has searched recently.

Check **How We Know** on a contact’s card for the source and age of the report. Your staff works from that same picture. Where the scenario offers a **Dry Run**, the staff can play a plan forward across several plausible situations and show how often it worked, what it cost, and what assumptions mattered. The estimate leaves the live game untouched. Read the assumptions before deciding what weight to give the result.

## A campaign runs on readiness

Ships burn fuel, carry cargo, and need time to rearm or repair. Air groups search, fly cover and strikes, and accumulate wear and fatigue. Troops have to reach shore and be fed. A carrier without a working flight deck or a base without ready aircraft changes what you can do even if both are still on the chart.

Over several days, those conditions shape the campaign. A convoy may matter because its cargo keeps an airfield in operation. A damaged ship may matter because it will miss the next engagement. Read the scenario’s mission before you set your priorities. Depending on the scenario, the final result can turn on what you landed, which bases you held or kept open, and what fighting strength you took out of action.

## Spend your attention where it matters

In scenarios that let you command within a day, **Next Pulse** takes you through the night, morning, and afternoon one at a time. Other scenarios resolve a full day at once. You can also choose what interrupts **Carry On**, such as a new contact, an attack, or a plan waiting for approval. You decide which developments warrant another look at the chart.

The **Evening Report** brings the day’s developments together. Follow a name to its card, open the plan behind an order, or read the **War Diary** to trace what happened. One day’s report might confirm that the base is still supplied. The next might force you to decide how much of the operation you can still afford.

## Battles to play and the longer vision

**The Eastern Solomons** offers a one-day carrier battle and a five-day scenario that brings the reinforcement convoy and the airfield at Lunga into play. **The Coral Sea** puts the landing at Port Moresby alongside the carrier fight. **Midway** gives you five days to balance the fate of the atoll and the fleets. **Guadalcanal: The First Thirty-Nine Days** gives you time to see how supply, readiness, and attrition compound.

Historical records inform the maps, forces, and scenarios. The project also runs batches of battles against the historical record and documents where the simulation diverges. Each scenario gives you a starting situation and objectives. Your decisions and the simulation determine how the battle turns out. [Historical validation](validation/step2-close.md)

The longer vision is to make this form of command work across a much larger campaign. The design imagines theatre, area, and force commanders each working from their own reports. A superior would hand down an objective and learn what happened as reports came back. A player could eventually choose which seat to occupy, with the game’s staff filling the others.
