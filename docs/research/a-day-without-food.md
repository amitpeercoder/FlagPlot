# A day without food

Read on 2026-11-21 for the architect, who is to set one figure: how much a unit fed nothing last night loses of its power to fight and to do hard work. The game now halves both at once. This note asks how much fighting and hard-work power falls after one day with no food, after two or three days and after about a week, compared with men who were fed. Each statement below is a row in `data/oob/land_unit_facts.csv`, ids 15026 to 15081. The rows' entities are `land_units.csv` 14916 to 14933 and their sources are `sources.csv` 14915 to 14929. The gaps are the last 6 entries of `gaps.csv`. It follows `hunger-and-recovery.md`, which covers weeks of short rations.

**In short:** the record has no measured study of soldiers after one to seven days with no food that dates from the war itself. The measured studies that exist are postwar American ones. In them, men with water and no food kept most of their capacity for hard work for days:

- **One day:** about 91 percent of maximal work time.
- **Four to ten days:** about 100 percent.
- **3.5 days:** strength and endurance unchanged or down 10 to 15 percent.

The exception is men who fasted while working hard at a high rate. A study of such men lost 40 percent of a fitness score in four days. That figure is second-hand, because the paper itself could not be got.

Every field case found mixes no food with no water, no sleep, short ammunition or defeat, and none gives a figure.

## Food, water and sleep told apart

- **Water comes first.** Hervey and McCance put a water deficit of 5 percent of body weight at a loss of efficiency, 10 percent at disabling and 20 percent at possibly lethal. They judge that two weeks without food does relatively slight harm (15047 to 15049). The Navy told its corpsmen that thirst caused the most suffering and deaths among survivors at sea, and that a man with water and no food lives about 21 days (15052, 15053). RAF survival doctrine put food fourth, after water (15051).
- **In the field, water was named apart from food only twice.** At Nhpum Ga the Marauders had food dropped regularly, and water was their "most desperate" want (15070). Kirby puts the first Chindit columns' exhaustion down to water and constant marching, not food (15076).
- **Sleep was never told apart from food.** Hackett names strain, sleep and food together (15079), and the 106th Evacuation Hospital names sleeplessness, hunger and fear together (15081). No study of the 1940s or 1950s that varied food and sleep separately was found.
- **Ammunition was named first where both ran out.** At Walawbum and Tingkrukawng the official history puts the withdrawal down to ammunition first, and to the day without food second (15067, 15068).
- **Keys's chapter 3:** total fasting is unlike long semi-starvation. Hunger "almost disappears after a few days" (15045).

## Where sources disagree

- **Christensen (via Forbes) and Henschel (via Consolazio) against Consolazio and Knapik.** Christensen and Henschel found that men fasting while working hard decline within two to four days: considerable deterioration in two days, and fitness scores down 40 percent in four. Consolazio's starved men, at 2,800 to 3,000 kcal a day of expenditure, kept their maximal work time for ten days. Knapik's men at rest lost little in 3.5 days. The two pairs do not necessarily contradict each other:
  - The pairs differ in how hard the men worked, and in the test. The Harvard-type fitness test is a run to exhaustion. Consolazio's test was a short maximal treadmill test.
  - Knapik himself writes that his result holds "provided the individual performs minimal physical activity".
  - The 40 percent comes second-hand, from a paper that was not read, and so does Christensen's statement.
- **Hervey and McCance against Forbes.** Hervey and McCance's "relatively slight" harm over two weeks without food is about survival and health at rest. Forbes's line is about men working hard.

## The reader's arithmetic (not fact)

This section is the researcher's reading of the rows above for the architect. It states no fact and settles nothing. Percentages are of full fighting and working power of a fed unit, read loosely from the measures of work capacity above, which are not measures of fighting.

- **After one night fed nothing: about 90 to 100 percent.** The only measured first day (Consolazio, 15028) gives 91 percent of maximal work time, not significant. Weygandt's one man did better at 24 hours than on control days. The Marauders' and the Raiders' days without food came with no water or no ammunition. Even so, the units attacked or took their objective. The record does not support half strength after one night. A loss of a tenth is the most it would bear, and only for hard physical work.
- **After three nights fed nothing: about 80 to 95 percent for hard physical work, nearer 100 for anything brief.** Knapik's men lost about 10 percent of one kind of strength and about 15 percent of endurance (not significant) at 3.5 days at rest. Consolazio's men were at 99 percent of maximal work time on day 4. Against this, men fasting while working hard fall faster. Christensen found considerable deterioration in two days, and Henschel a 40 percent fall in a fitness score in four. A unit that marched or dug hard through three unfed days would sit at the low end, and possibly below it.
- **After seven nights fed nothing: about 70 to 100 percent, with the widest doubt.** Consolazio's starved men were at 107 percent of maximal work time on day 7 and 100 on day 10. Their oxygen uptake began to fall only from day 5 to 9, and their heart records were abnormal by day 10. They did not fight or march. No field case of a full week with no food at all was found, and every week-long case is mixed with water, sleep and disease. The figure should fall with the work done. A unit that marched and fought hard for a week unfed is not in any measured row. Henschel's 40 percent at four days of hard work suggests it would lose more than a resting unit.
- **What this says about the rule.** The measures point to a fall that is small and slow while the men rest and faster when they work hard. They also suggest that water, sleep and ammunition, not food, are what break a unit within a day or two. A rule that takes power from a unit by the count of nights unfed, and by how hard it worked, would sit closer to the record than one that halves it after one night. The rows give no figure for the shape of that fall beyond the points above.
