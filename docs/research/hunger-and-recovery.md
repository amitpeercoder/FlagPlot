# Hunger and recovery, 1942 to 1945

Read on 2026-11-21 for the architect, who is to write how a hungry unit recovers (decision 0205, rules chapter 7, "The men's supply"). It asks two things of the record: how long hunger took to keep soldiers from fighting, and how long they took to be fit again once fed. Each statement below is a row in `data/oob/land_unit_facts.csv`, ids 14900 to 15025. Every quotation in those rows was compared by program with a fresh download of the page it cites, after the researchers had checked it once; all 126 were found word for word (spaces aside).

## C. A difference between the sides

- **The Japanese ration was smaller by design.** On Bataan 62 ounces against 71 for Americans and 64 for Filipinos (Morton, rows 14901 and 14922); TM-E 30-480 says the Japanese soldier's needs are "considerably less" (15025) and puts his ration at about two-thirds of the American (row 1037, on file).
- **Wartime American claims, with no figure behind them:** Japanese training aims at withstanding hunger and fatigue (15024); the Japanese soldier would starve or be shot rather than surrender (15023). A Japanese general told his men in December that "patient endurance of starvation" would be rewarded (14929).
- **What both sides did.** Starving Japanese held the Gifu (14932) and Sanananda (14946) for weeks, but so did the starving Americans and Filipinos on Bataan for three months, and the Australians went on fighting on the trail. On every side starving men held positions; none mounted a successful attack. The record shows no case of one side recovering faster than the other.

## D. Gaps

The last 12 entries of `gaps.csv`. In short:

- **A:** no share of Japanese troops fit by date and unit on Guadalcanal; no Australian ration in grams; no hospital admissions for hunger apart from malaria on Bataan. Almost every field figure mixes hunger with malaria and dysentery: the clean ones are Bataan before March and the Minnesota Experiment.
- **B:** no time to fitness for hunger alone for any fighting force on either side. The 1st Marine Division's, the 32d Division's and the Australians' recovery is put down to malaria. Nothing on the Japanese units taken off Guadalcanal beyond the 2d Division's move to the Philippines. Nothing on Bataan's survivors. Keys gives the measures week by week but no number of weeks "fit for hard work".
- **C:** no measured difference in how long either side fought on less, or recovered.

## Where sources disagree

- The Army's medical history says the Minnesota men were starved "some 3 to 4 months" (Youmans ch. V, the passage of row 14998, not filed as a row of its own); the book itself says 24 weeks (14969). The book is the authors' own report and is followed here.
- Bataan's combat efficiency in mid-March: 45 percent for the force (doctors, 14912), 20 percent for II Corps (Parker, 14913), 75 percent unfit in I Corps (Wainwright, 14914). They are of different bodies and are not settled against each other.
- The 34 percent unfit of one regiment at relief is the same examination in Hough and in the division's report (14999, 15000); they agree.

## The reader's arithmetic (not fact)

This section is the researcher's reading of the rows above for the architect. It states no fact and settles nothing.

- **The fall is slow.** On Bataan the first sign was a month on half rations (listless men, battalions needing relief after five days); a fighting force fell to about half its efficiency in about ten weeks on a third to a half of a ration, and towards nothing at twelve, with malaria telling from about the eighth week. The rule's fourteen nights before men die sit well inside that.
- **The rise is slower than the fall for long hunger.** After 24 weeks of hunger the Minnesota men had back almost nothing in 3 weeks, a third of what they had lost in 12, and seven tenths in 20; strength came last, at about 33 weeks. Recovery came faster the more they were fed: at 12 weeks the two higher steps had back about half again as much as the +400 step, and more than twice as much as the lowest.
- **The rise is quick for short hunger.** The Army's experience was that men bore days or weeks of slightly short rations if they were well fed when they came out of the line.
- **A figure the rows would support.** Full fighting strength after N nights of hunger needs about N nights of full feeding, with nothing gained in the first few, and about 1.5 N for long hunger of many weeks. One way to have it with the state the game already keeps is to let the count of nights of hunger fall by one for each fully fed night, as it does, and to scale a unit's fighting strength by its count rather than by last night's fed share alone, so that one good night after a fortnight's hunger gives back only a fourteenth of what was lost.
- **No different figure for the Japanese side.** The record gives the Japanese a smaller ration by design, which the daily charge per man can carry if a scenario sets it by side. It gives no faster recovery and no longer endurance for one side than the other; both sides held while starving and neither attacked well. Nothing here supports a separate Japanese figure for recovery.
- **A caution.** The field figures mix hunger with malaria, which in the record took longer to mend than hunger (three to six months for the 1st Marine Division) and which the game does not model. The Minnesota men had none of it and did not fight.
