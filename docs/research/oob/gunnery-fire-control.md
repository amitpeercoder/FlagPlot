# Surface gunnery fire control, 1942-43

Status: research note. Design input for scenarios 3 to 5; it proposes nothing binding.

How to read it: drafted by a research subagent from pages read on 2026-11-10, then reviewed. Each statement carries its source. The Navy's own history site would not load that day, so some points rest on Wikipedia or a single website; those are marked "unverified", "W" or "WP lead" and should be checked against a primary source before a rule relies on them.

## Peacetime baseline and optics

- Night doctrine: open on an estimated range and correct from fall of shot, using ranging ladders rather than a refined solution. [USN Night Combat in 1942, WarHistory.org (author not named), 2018/2024, https://warhistory.org/ko/article/usn-night-combat-in-1942-gunnery-radars-and-littoral-terrain]
- Peacetime reference point: Augusta scored 38 hits (42.2%) on a 1937 night run. [same, WarHistory.org] Treat as a drill result, not combat.
- Night visual range off Guadalcanal was nearly always under 10,000 yd and often under 5,000. [same]
- Japanese lookouts and large-aperture binoculars gave a pre-radar edge. [Haley, Military Strategy Magazine, 2026, https://www.militarystrategymagazine.com/article/adaptation-in-ambiguity-the-battle-of-cape-esperance-11-12-october-1942/] Specific binocular models and apertures: unverified.

## Computers and director control

- The Mark 1 computer used synchro transmission, had no memory, and needed up to about 30 seconds to solve a moving target. Range and bearing could be entered manually when there was no sight line. [Mark 1 Fire Control Computer, Slover, NavWeaps, https://www.navweaps.com/index_tech/tech-056.php]
- With radar, range feeds the rangekeeper automatically and the operator can adjust course and speed inputs to keep the radar tracking. The director can also be trained by radar for blind fire. [Naval Ordnance and Gunnery Vol. 2, ch. 20, hosted by Slover, https://eugeneleeslover.com/USNAVY/CHAPTER-20-G.html]
- Remote power control: not covered by any page read (unverified). Power faults did matter: South Dakota lost fire-control power about 0033-0036 on 14-15 Nov, and one Washington secondary mount fired wild when its training motor kicked out. [ONI Combat Narrative VI, Part III, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Guadalcanal/USN-CN-Guadalcanal-3.html]

## Radar ranging and spotting

- Mark 3: reliable to about 28,000 yd on battleships, ranging within about 40 yd, but poor for spotting except range. Mark 8: reliable to 40,000 yd, spots both range and deflection through splashes. [FTP 217, 1943, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/ref/Radar/Radar-2.html]
- Some ships thought FC/FD good enough for blind fire, though radar range with visual bearing was most accurate. [WarHistory.org]
- Washington, 0016, 14-15 Nov: opened at 18,500 yd on radar ranges before being lit; 42 rounds of 16-inch in three minutes, first salvo reported as straddling. [ONI VI, Part III]
- At Kirishima (8,400 yd) Washington fired 75 rounds of 16-inch and took about eight hits on the target. [ONI VI, Part III]

## Rates of fire and what was achieved

- 6"/47 cruiser guns: 8 to 10 rounds per minute. [6"/47 Mark 16, NavWeaps, https://navweaps.com/Weapons/WNUS_6-47_mk16.php] 8"/55: 3 to 4. [8"/55 Mark 9, NavWeaps, https://navweaps.com/Weapons/WNUS_8-55_mk9.php]
- No fetched source gives a combat night hit percentage. Outcomes: Hiei took about 85 hits from cruisers and destroyers on 12-13 Nov. [USSBS Interrogation Nav 46 (Yunoki), 1945, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/AAF/USSBS/IJO/IJO-46.html] At Tassafaronga no Japanese shell hit a US ship in phase one. [ONI Combat Narrative VII, 1943, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Tassafaronga/index.html]

## Flash, illumination, searchlights

- Washington's own 5-inch flashes blinded main-battery spotters, and star shells that fell short did too. Japanese flashes looked small, suggesting flashless powder or hiders. [ONI VI, Part III]
- Flashless charges are listed for 6"/47 and 8"/55, but whether they were used in 1942 is not stated. [NavWeaps pages above]
- Japanese searchlight work on 12-13 Nov was rated outstanding, lit from both sides. [ONI Combat Narrative VI, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Guadalcanal/USN-CN-Guadalcanal-1.html] Hiei lit up and drew concentrated fire. [Yunoki, 1945]
- Wright banned searchlights at Tassafaronga; the plane flares came too late, so starshells were used. [ONI VII]
- Japanese practice: torpedoes first, guns after, using enemy gun flashes as aim points. [WarHistory.org]

## Friendly fire and confusion

- Cape Esperance: Scott's cease-fire orders and the "Roger" episode; Farenholt suffered heavy friendly fire. [Haley, 2026; ONI Combat Narrative IV, ch. 6, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Esperance/USN-CN-Esperance-6.html]
- 12-13 Nov: Monssen was lit by searchlights she thought friendly. [ONI VI]
- Tassafaronga: Lardner and Lamson were fired on by friendly ships. [ONI VII]

## Change over time

Mark 8 and later sets (Mark 13) could see individual large-caliber splashes at long range. [Radar WWII, NavWeaps, https://navweaps.com/Weapons/WNUS_Radar_WWII.php] CIC role in feeding computers: unverified beyond the manual-input note in Slover. Evidence for 1944-45 is thin here.

## What a rule would need to model

- Visibility range at night versus radar range, and which is limiting.
- Quality of director, computer and radar integration per ship class.
- Radar capable of ranging only, or also spotting splashes.
- Time to get a solution and the penalty for a maneuvering target.
- Illumination (starshell, searchlight, flares) versus the cost of revealing yourself.
- Flash and smoke effects on own spotters and on enemy aim.
- Rate of fire versus ammunition and magazine exposure.
- Power or equipment failure under fire.
- Identification, recognition signals and risk of friendly fire.
- Torpedo-first versus gun-first doctrine.
