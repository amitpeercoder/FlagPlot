# Damage control, Aug 1942 - Feb 1943

Status: research note. Design input for scenarios 3 to 5; it proposes nothing binding.

How to read it: drafted by a research subagent from pages read on 2026-11-10, then reviewed. Each statement carries its source. The Navy's own history site would not load that day, so some points rest on Wikipedia or a single website; those are marked "unverified", "W" or "WP lead" and should be checked against a primary source before a rule relies on them.

## What sank or saved ships: US

- Savo cruisers. BuShips judged that Quincy and Vincennes were so heavily holed that loss was certain. Astoria's structural damage alone was survivable; uncontrolled fires lost her. Fires spread from topside paint, life jackets, flags, aircraft, boats, oil and kerosene, and in Astoria's wardroom area from paint, records and excess gear. The fire worked down to the 5-inch hoists and magazine, which blew a hole below the waterline, and she capsized. [Bureau of Ships War Damage Report No. 29, USN, 1943, ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/rep/WDR/ (PDF), Summary and Discussion B]
- Aviation fuel is not the Savo story in this report. Four of the five aircraft per ship had been drained of gasoline, and the report found no gasoline fires at the tanks. Ready-service 5-inch ammunition did explode, and fire-main risers were cut by hits, leaving no pressure. [same, Narrative para 9, Discussion B-C]
- Canberra. About 24 hits in under two minutes; 84 killed including the captain. [Sinking of HMAS Canberra, Australian War Memorial, n.d., awm.gov.au/encyclopedia/ww2_navy/savo/] She was left helpless and burning, magazines flooded, with a list of about 17 to 20 degrees. Bucket brigades and hoses from a destroyer did not stop the fire. She was scuttled by US destroyers about 0800 (a torpedo from Ellet finished her). [Battle of Savo Island, ONI Combat Narrative, USN, chapter "End of the Canberra", ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Savo/USN-CN-Savo-8.html] The AWM summary says "the next day"; the ONI timing is the morning of the 9th. Power loss: not found in sources read (unverified).
- Wasp, 15 Sep 1942. Two torpedoes hit while aircraft were being fuelled and armed, so much of the gasoline system was live. Gasoline from ruptured tanks and mains burned, and hangar fires started when aircraft fell onto others. Vapour explosions followed; hangar-deck water pressure was very low, which BuShips traced to an unisolated break in the forward fire-main loop. Abandoned in about 35 minutes. BuShips said hull damage was not fatal. [BuShips War Damage Report, Wasp (CV-7), 1943, ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/rep/WDR/ (PDF), Summary]
- Hornet, 26 Oct 1942. Three bombs, two torpedoes and two suicide crashes; fires were under control in about an hour, but the forward engine room flooded and all power, communications and fire-main pressure were lost. A tow succeeded briefly; a further torpedo hit ended hope of power and she was abandoned at 18 degrees list. BuShips said an Essex-type machinery layout might have kept power. Fires fed on excess clothing, upholstery and ready-room material. [BuShips War Damage Report No. 30, Hornet (CV-8), 1943, ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/WarDamageReports/WarDamageReportCV8/, Summary and Section I]
- Enterprise. Eastern Solomons: three bomb hits, 74 killed; damage control patched her and she steamed to Pearl Harbor, where repairs ran 10 Sep to 16 Oct. Santa Cruz: two bomb hits, 44 killed, she stayed in action; repairs at Noumea began 30 Oct, interrupted by a sortie on 11 Nov. A steering casualty is not in this source (unverified). [USS Enterprise (CV-6), DANFS, ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/ships/dafs/CV/cv6.html]
- Saratoga (31 Aug torpedo): short circuits stopped her turbo-electric plant; an improvised circuit restored 10 knots the same day; six days of repair at Tongatabu, then Pearl Harbor from 21 Sep to a 10 Nov departure. North Carolina (15 Sep): list corrected within minutes and she kept 26 knots; repair dates not given. [DANFS entries CV-3 and BB-55, ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/ships/dafs/CV/cv3.html, .../BB/bb55.html]

## US practice and changes

- BuShips' 1943 programme after Savo: more pumps with independent power, fire mains split into loops, fog nozzles, and essential flammables moved below the waterline and to the ends of the ship with CO2 smothering. Comcrupac issued a directive in September 1942 on removing flammables from cruisers; Captains could fly off or dump aircraft. [WDR No. 29, Summary paras 6-7; Discussion B]
- Carriers: the Wasp report describes inert-gas protection for fuel-tank voids, mains blown empty, a 25 Feb 1943 BuShips fire-fighting letter, a 1943 Firefighting Manual, and gasoline-handling and fire-fighting schools. [Wasp report, Sections III, 55-56]
- Hornet report: diesel fire pumps, aircraft defuelling connections and portable "casualty power" leads. [Hornet report, recommendations]

## Japanese cases

- Ryujo (24 Aug): four bombs and a torpedo; fire was put out but list reached 21 degrees and flooding stopped machinery. Abandoned about 75 minutes after the hits. [IJN Ryujo TROM, combinedfleet.com/ryujo.htm]
- Shokaku (26 Oct): four to six bombs; no fuel was active, damage control saved her after under five hours; about 80 hangar personnel and many gunners died. [IJN Shokaku TROM, combinedfleet.com/shokaku.htm]
- Hiei: two shells flooded the steering room and shorted power steering; she was steered manually until the crew had to leave the compartment, then circled. Air attacks all day; two torpedoes at 1435 re-flooded the steering room. Tully argues gunfire started the loss, air attack finished it, and scuttling was probably premature. [Death of Battleship HIEI, A. Tully, 1997, combinedfleet.com/atully03.htm]
- Kirishima: steering compartment flooded, rudders jammed, most fires out, but flooding and list capsized her at 0325. Furutaka: about 90 shell hits, fires and a torpedo; sank by 0228. Kako: three torpedoes, gone in seven minutes. [combinedfleet.com Kirishima.htm, furuta_t.htm, kako_t.htm]

## Japanese practice and the contested story

- NHHC's H-gram (Cox) says Japanese damage control was a small specialist cadre, often killed early, with no damage-control conditions or central, and enclosed hangars where aircraft were fuelled, and that Japan did not spread the Shokaku lessons. [H-005-2, S. J. Cox, NHHC, 2017/2019, navyleaguewestct.org/Battle of Midway Carrier to Carrier.pdf]
- US Naval Technical Mission report S-84(N) "Japanese Damage Control" and S-06 "Reports of Damage to Japanese Warships" exist, but they were not read, only their titles. USSBS: nothing found. [US Naval Technical Mission to Japan, report list, ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/rep/NTM/NTM.html]
- Contested: the Hiei, Shokaku and Ryujo accounts show long, effective effort by Japanese crews, and a 1944 account rates Shokaku's damage control above Taiho's. No source read gave a historian directly disputing the "poor" label in a fetched page (unverified). [Tully, IJNH, 2002, quoting Y'Blood 1981]

## Later contrast

Taiho and Shokaku (June 1944) died of vapour explosions after torpedo hits. [Tully 2002] Franklin (March 1945): 724 killed, but survived and sailed home. [Franklin, DANFS, ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/ships/dafs/CV/cv13.html] Bunker Hill and Shinano: not fetched.

## What a rule would need to model

- Fuel state at the moment of hit: aircraft fuelled or drained, gasoline lines live or filled with inert gas.
- Flammable load and its removal over time.
- Fire-main and power redundancy: loops, independent pumps, casualty power.
- Hull damage and fire as separate loss paths.
- Steering and propulsion as separate casualties that decide whether a damaged ship escapes.
- Crew training spread across the ship, plus losses of specialists.
- Whether a tow or salvage is possible given the tactical situation.
- Repair location and time: tender, advance base, Pearl Harbor, home yard.
- Doctrine change lag: when lessons reach which ships.
