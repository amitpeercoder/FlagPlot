# Radar in the Solomons, 1942-43

Status: research note. Design input for scenarios 3 to 5; it proposes nothing binding.

How to read it: drafted by a research subagent from pages read on 2026-11-10, then reviewed. Each statement carries its source. The Navy's own history site would not load that day, so some points rest on Wikipedia or a single website; those are marked "unverified", "W" or "WP lead" and should be checked against a primary source before a rule relies on them.

## Sets and who had them

- US search sets: CXAM (A-scope only, about 25,000 yd on a large ship), SC (A-scope; PPI only on SC-2/3), and microwave SG (PPI plus A-scope, about 15 miles on large ships, bearing within 2 degrees). One operator per shift. Note this manual is dated August 1943, so figures may reflect later models. [U.S. Radar: Operational Characteristics (FTP 217), Joint Committee on New Weapons, 1943, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/ref/Radar/Radar-1.html]
- US fire-control sets: Mark 3 (FC) ranged within about 40 yd but was not suited to spotting except in range; Mark 8 could also spot deflection by seeing splashes. [FTP 217, 1943, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/ref/Radar/Radar-2.html]
- Japan: little shipboard radar, and none attested in the surface actions. A member of the Japanese force at Savo Island said they had no radar. [USSBS Interrogation Nav 61 (Matsuyama), 1945, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/AAF/USSBS/IJO/IJO-61.html] Hiei had none on 12-13 Nov 1942. [USSBS Interrogation Nav 46 (Yunoki), 1945, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/AAF/USSBS/IJO/IJO-46.html] Aoba had no active radar at Cape Esperance, and Haley puts the first firm evidence of Japanese shipborne radar at a November 1943 photo of Haguro. [Haley, Military Strategy Magazine, 2026, https://www.militarystrategymagazine.com/article/adaptation-in-ambiguity-the-battle-of-cape-esperance-11-12-october-1942/]
- That last claim is contradicted for carriers and battleships. Shokaku's movement record says an early Type 21 air search set was fitted during repairs ending 27 June 1942, and that her radar room detected incoming aircraft on 24 August 1942, though the warning did not reach the bridge in time. [Shokaku tabular record, combinedfleet.com, https://www.combinedfleet.com/shokaku.htm] The same site records experimental sets on Ise and Hyuga from spring 1942, with dates that differ between its own pages. See `conflicts.md`.
- Type 22 surface search fits and radar detectors (E27): unverified. No fetched page gave firm dates or ships. A Wikipedia list (lead only) shows land-based 13-Go entering service August 1943. [List of Japanese WWII radars, Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Japanese_World_War_II_radars]

## Real limits

- Land clutter near islands hid or merged contacts, which surprised the Navy and shook prewar confidence. [USN Night Combat in 1942, WarHistory.org (author not named), 2018/2024, https://warhistory.org/ko/article/usn-night-combat-in-1942-gunnery-radars-and-littoral-terrain]
- Tassafaronga: Northampton's CXAM could not pick contacts from the Guadalcanal land mass, which also blanketed the rear destroyers' radars. [ONI Combat Narrative VII, 1943, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Tassafaronga/index.html]
- A-scope showed one bearing at a time. The PPI gave a picture but few ships could share it. [WarHistory.org, as above]
- Switching sets off: Haley says Scott kept the flagship's SC inactive at Cape Esperance for fear of detection, and that Japanese detection of SC was never shown. [Haley, 2026, as above] The ONI narrative's engagement chapter records no such order. [ONI Combat Narrative IV, 1943, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Esperance/USN-CN-Esperance-6.html] Disagreement noted.
- Tassafaronga plan kept CXAM, SC and SG-1 silent except on the guardship. [ONI VII, as above]
- Trust and training: Scott doubted Boise's reports and read Helena's request to fire as a request to acknowledge, replying "Roger". [Haley, 2026]
- Flagship without SG: at Guadalcanal on 12-13 Nov, San Francisco had none, and Callaghan had no radar of his own on the approach. [ONI Combat Narrative VI, 1944, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Guadalcanal/USN-CN-Guadalcanal-1.html]
- Voice delay: Helena's 0124 SG contacts reached Callaghan by TBS in about six minutes, during which the forces closed roughly four miles. [ONI VI, as above]

## Episodes

- Savo, 8-9 Aug 1942: Blue patrolled west of Savo at 12 knots, Talbot north. [ONI Combat Narrative I, 1943, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Savo/USN-CN-Savo-2.html] Mikawa passed Blue about 0132 unseen. [Bates and Innis, Naval War College, 1950, https://www.ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/rep/Savo/NWC/NWC-Savo-Fwd.html] ONI only speculates that the pickets searched intermittently or that the enemy hugged the shore, both unproven. [ONI I, ch. 11, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Savo/USN-CN-Savo-11.html] Astoria's radar was swamped by land on most bearings. [ONI I, ch. 3, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Savo/USN-CN-Savo-3.html]
- Cape Esperance, 11-12 Oct: Boise and Helena had SG; San Francisco had only SC-1. [Haley, 2026] Boise's SG antenna wire fouled about a minute after she opened fire. [ONI IV, ch. 6, as above] Time and range of first SG contact differ by source (unverified).
- Guadalcanal, 12-13 Nov: only five ships had SG. Radar ranges were reliable, but the fight was not conducted to radar's advantage. [ONI VI, as above]
- Guadalcanal, 14-15 Nov: Washington opened fire at 18,500 yd on radar ranges before being illuminated. [ONI VI, Part III, https://ibiblio.org/hyperwar/USN/USN-CN-Guadalcanal/USN-CN-Guadalcanal-3.html]
- Tassafaronga, 30 Nov: SG coverage uneven, and Fletcher and Perkins gave conflicting bearings that were never reconciled. [ONI VII, as above]

## After 1942

Japanese reliance on trained lookouts and big binoculars gave an edge until US radar and doctrine matured. [Haley, 2026] The CIC grew from radar plot after a late-1942 bulletin and a January 1943 renaming (unverified; search snippet only). Mark 8 fire-control radar could spot splashes at long range. [FTP 217, 1943] Coverage of 1943-45 here is thin.

## What a rule would need to model

- Set type, band and display (A-scope versus PPI) per ship, not a single "has radar" flag.
- Which ship carries the best search set, and whether that is the flagship.
- Land and coast clutter by geography and bearing.
- Operator and crew training, and commander trust in reports.
- Emission policy (silent versus active) versus counter-detection fear.
- Delay and loss when reports pass by voice between ships.
- Sharing of the picture, and later a plot or CIC to fuse it.
- Gaps in picket coverage: patrol pattern, speed, intermittent search.
- Japanese lookout and optics quality as the counterpart, and its decline against radar.
