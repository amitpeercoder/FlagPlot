# What the record gives to rest an "experience" figure on, 1942

Status: research report for the architect, read on 2026-11-12. Nothing here is settled. Rows: `air_unit_facts.csv` ids 342 to 431 (the air groups of the scenarios), `ship_facts.csv` ids 931 to 1059 (the ships at Savo Island and Cape Esperance), and a new file `training_facts.csv` (86 rows: statements by navy and period), with 71 new gaps. Seven quotations were checked a second time against saved pages or the text of the 1950 Savo analysis; the rest are as the researchers recorded them.

## Disputes and gaps first

**Disputes**
- **Flying hours of Japanese naval pilots at the start of the war.** Five figures. The post-war bombing survey's summary: "Navy pilots 650 hours" before entering combat (checked). A website quoting the survey's fuller report: 700 on average. The same website quoting a Japanese officer: 800 on average for the Pearl Harbor pilots, from 300 to 2,500. A Marine squadron history: "approximately 800", no source. Only the first was read at the source.
- **Hours in the Japanese flying course.** 150, 330, or 160 and 104 by rank followed by 150 of tactical training, all through the one website. Course length 7, 9 or 12 months.
- **Whether the Japanese cruisers at Savo Island had trained together.** The 1950 analysis, from Japanese officials: "they had never engaged in maneuvers together" (checked). A modern compilation of ship records has two of the three divisions sailing together in March and April 1942 and training together in June.
- **Scott's practice before Cape Esperance.** One night battle practice on 8 October (Wikipedia); target practice on 8 October and anti-aircraft practice that night (the 1943 narrative); night firing on 7 October with San Francisco and Boise (Salt Lake City's deck log as transcribed). The "two to three weeks of night drills" often quoted was found in no source worth a row.
- **VMF-224's first combat.** 31 August or 2 September.

**Gaps**
- **US Navy pilots' hours on joining a squadron in 1942: not found anywhere.**
- No dated figure for the Japanese replacement pilot of mid or late 1942.
- No veteran share in numbers for any unit on either side.
- No formation or first-combat dates for the US carrier squadrons (VF-5, VF-6, VB-3, VB-6, VS-3, VS-5, VT-3) or the Japanese carrier groups beyond "since December 1941".
- **No crew-experience figure for any ship.** Nothing on the share of a crew with under a year's service, gunnery scores, battle efficiency awards or hours of night steaming.
- Japanese ships: almost nothing beyond refit dates; nothing on enlistment or gunnery allowances.
- Not read: the bombing survey's report on Japanese air power, the Japanese technical mission's report on damage control, the second fleet bulletin on battle experience, the inquiry into Canberra's loss.

**Statements by navy** (in `training_facts.csv`):
- **US night training.** The 1950 analysis: "practically none of the ships" of the amphibious force "had engaged in any night battle exercise since the beginning of the war" (checked). The fleet bulletin after Cape Esperance: Salt Lake City "had trained in night star shell duels"; the destroyers' mixed origins "precluded coordinated action".
- **Japanese night training.** The 1950 analysis: "The Japanese had placed great stress in their fleet training on night attack procedures" (checked). It also says their recent night firing is only assumed.
- **Damage control, US.** The 1950 analysis lists inexperience, excess paint and missing fog nozzles at Savo Island; the war damage report on the three cruisers says inflammable material fed the fires; the fleet bulletin credits Boise and Salt Lake City with "excellent training and damage control organization" in October; by November paint and linoleum had been stripped from a battleship.
- **Damage control, Japan.** One statement, at second hand: a 1948 summary of the US technical mission's findings says Japanese "organisation, training, and equipment were inferior". The mission's own report was not found.
- **Readiness measures of the period.** Two: a 1941 schedule giving one night general quarters a week in one ship, and the rounds fired in her night practice of 7 October 1942.

## What could carry a number, and what could not

**Could, from facts that do not depend on a battle's result:**
- For an air group: months since first combat; flying hours on joining, by navy and year, where a source gives it; named losses before the scenario date.
- For a ship: days in commission; days since the last yard period; days in the theatre; days since the captain changed; whether she had exercised at night with her group before the battle, and how recently.

**Could not:**
- Any share of veterans or of recruits: no source gives one.
- "Well trained", "experienced", "very inexperienced": words. Several of the statements about the Japanese were written after the battle by the side that lost it, and should not feed a figure.
- A comparison of damage control: one second-hand sentence for Japan against detailed US self-criticism. The US record shows a change between August and October 1942 that could be dated; the Japanese side cannot be placed on the same scale from what was read.

`training_facts.csv` is new and `oob-check` does not read it. Its entities are navies and arms of service (`ijn-aviators`, `ijn-carrier-aviators`, `ijn-land-based-aviators`, `usn-aviators`, `usmc-aviators`, `usaaf-fighter-pilots`, `ijn-cruiser-destroyer-crews`, `ijn-surface-fleet`, `usn-cruiser-destroyer-crews`, `usn-surface-fleet`, `ran-cruiser-crews`); its attributes are `flying_hours_on_joining`, `flying_hours_average_in_unit`, `training_course_months`, `training_hours_in_course`, `pilots_trained_per_year`, `carrier_qualification_note`, `night_training_note`, `service_length_note`, `gunnery_practice_note`, `lookout_training_note`, `damage_control_note`, `training_note`.

## A second source on Japanese night training (12 November 2026)

Asked for by the build session for decision 0064, which admits a statement about a navy as a whole only with a second source. Six rows, `training_facts.csv` ids 87 to 92, all `unconfirmed`. Each quote was copied from a downloaded copy of the page and I read each in its context.

**The second source exists, and it is Japanese testimony.** Vice Admiral Ozawa, asked by the Strategic Bombing Survey in 1945 why Japan put so much emphasis on night operations, answered that the night battle favours the side with the weaker force, "and so we stressed training on that type of battle from before the war" (row 87). It does not rest on the 1950 analysis that row 32 quotes. Its limits: one officer's memory, in answer to a question that already assumed the emphasis; it is about the navy as a whole and names no ship, squadron, exercise or date.

**One statement gives a length of practice, for destroyers.** Captain Toyama, chief of staff of the 2nd Destroyer Squadron at Tassafaronga, said of his divisions' simultaneous column turn: "We had practiced this turn for about a year and one-half at night" (row 88). That is one manoeuvre of one destroyer squadron, which was at neither Savo Island nor Cape Esperance. The Survey's own campaign history repeats it and is not a further source.

**A Japanese-authored history shows night action shaped the ships, not that crews trained for it.** Japanese Monograph 145 says the navy "required their destroyers to carry heavy torpedo equipment to be used for night combat" (row 92).

**Three statements limit the claim, all for late 1943.** Vice Admiral Omori put his collisions at Empress Augusta Bay down to "lack of training in night operations", and said substitute ships from other fleets "never had an opportunity to train together" (rows 89 to 91). They show the skill was not permanent or uniform. They say nothing of August to October 1942.

**Nothing was found against the claim for 1942,** and nothing that confines the training to the battle fleet's destroyers or says it lapsed after December 1941.

**Nothing was found on any named ship.** No source read gives a night firing or torpedo exercise, or the lack of one, for Chokai, the 6th Cruiser Division, Tenryu, Yubari, Yunagi, Fubuki or Hatsuyuki. The one Savo Island interrogation online is of a rear admiral who was a guest passenger in Tenryu, and it does not touch training. So the evidence for these ships stays what it was: Bates's statement that the Savo cruisers "had never maneuvered together before" (row 35) and that their recent night firing is assumed, not shown (row 34).

**Where to look next** (in `gaps.csv`): the war diaries of the 6th and 18th Cruiser Divisions in the Japanese national archives, which may log exercises by date; the interrogations of Chokai's executive officer and the 6th Cruiser Division's chief of staff, which are missing from the online copy; and a 1952 Naval Institute article that could not be opened.

Also read, with nothing usable: the Naval Technical Mission to Japan reports O-29, O-31, O-32, O-38 and E-29, and the Navy's combat narratives of Savo Island and Cape Esperance.

## The cruiser divisions' own war diaries, and four follow-ups (12 November 2026)

Asked for by the build session for decisions 0064 and 0068. Rows: `ship_facts.csv` ids 1060 to 1122, `engagement_events.csv` ids 718 to 725, two new sources, four gaps.

### What was read

Thirteen scanned volumes from the Japanese national archives, about 700 handwritten pages, by four researchers working by eye: the 6th Cruiser Division's war diary and action reports for December 1941 to October 1942 (six volumes), and the 18th Cruiser Division's for May to early September 1942 (seven volumes). I read the pages behind every row graded medium; rows graded low say that I did not.

### The 6th Cruiser Division (Aoba, Kinugasa, Kako, Furutaka)

| When | What the diary records | By night? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4 and 9 February 1942 | Main battery firing with a rangefinder drill; a tracking drill | Not said |
| 3 March, 26 March, about 9 April | "Battle drill", numbered first to third | Not said |
| 20 April | Gunnery practice out of Truk, back the same day | No: the diary calls it daytime |
| 20 and 21 May | Bomb-avoidance drill by one section | Not said |
| June (home waters to 15 June) | Repair and refit at Kure, then the passage to Truk; no training in the narrative | Nothing recorded; the day-by-day table is missing |
| 14 to 16 July | First anti-aircraft practice firing of the half-year; battle drill on two days | Not said |
| 4 September | A main battery firing drill at Kieta | Not said |
| 3, 5 and 9 October | Live firing training for the airfield bombardment, and gunnery study daily | Not said |

**No night gunnery, night torpedo or night battle exercise is recorded in any month,** and no exercise with the 18th Division. That is an absence in a diary with holes (December and January abridged, late May and the June table missing), and "battle drill" may hide a night drill. It shows the diary does not record night training. It does not show there was none.

### The 18th Cruiser Division (Tenryu, Tatsuta)

- **Home waters, 23 May to 15 June:** docked and under maintenance at Maizuru; a new division commander on 13 June; no training recorded.
- **The passage to Truk, 15 to 22 June:** the order for it schedules a searchlight drill on the evening of 15 June, joint range-taking drills, battle training on three days and a night formation-keeping test. The diary records one-round main gun firings and "various underway training" on those days, without saying which.
- **With the 6th Division:** the two met on 18 June and sailed together, and were in one force from 24 June. Neither diary records a joint drill. This bears on the dispute between Bates ("had never maneuvered together", rows 1023 to 1029) and a web page (training together in the Bungo Strait, rows 1030 and 1031): the diaries show a passage in company and nothing more.
- **July and August:** operations only.

**The division's lessons after Savo Island** (rows 1121 and 1122) are its own judgement of its night fighting, and I read them on the page:

- Gunnery: "still more earnest, intensive training" is needed on prompt opening of fire and rate of fire.
- Lookout: an enemy destroyer about 2 km away did not notice the force, "the main cause" of the surprise; and attention fixes on the nearest enemy.
- Searchlights drew fire but were "extremely effective" for telling friend from foe; the enemy never used them.

The first of these limits the general statements in `training_facts.csv` rows 32 and 87.

### Whether each drill was at night

Every row says so in its value. Only two entries are marked: the firing of 20 April is by day, and the 18th Division's searchlight drill and formation test of June are at night but are orders. For everything else the diary does not say.

### Destroyer Squadron 4 and Scott's destroyers (rows 1060 to 1085)

- Gill lists nine destroyers in the 4th Destroyer Squadron on 7 July 1942: Selfridge, Henley, Mugford, Blue, Helm, Patterson, Ralph Talbot, Jarvis and Bagley. Only Patterson, Ralph Talbot and Jarvis left Brisbane with the force on 14 July.
- The ship dictionary puts Helm with the force at Brisbane from 19 May and Bagley there from 30 May; it has Blue on the Hawaii to San Francisco run until June and Ralph Talbot in Hawaii until 14 June; it disagrees with Gill on Patterson (left Brisbane 22 June) and Jarvis (at Sydney). Those two pairs are `disputed`.
- No source names a destroyer in the night training Crutchley mentions.
- Scott's force: Farenholt, Buchanan and Laffey were with it on 7 and 8 October. Duncan joined on the 10th. McCalla was sent back on the night of the 7th and rejoined on the 11th. That the last two missed the practice of 8 October is my inference from those dates.

### The Japanese force at Cape Esperance (events 718 to 725)

From the 6th Cruiser Division's own action report, which I read on the page. Times are Tokyo time as printed.

- **What it expected:** it judged that a counterattack by a large enemy surface force "had almost no chance", because enemy ships left the area at night.
- **Lookout:** with no enemy reports from the day's search or from the reinforcement group ahead, "an especially strict lookout was being kept".
- **Formation:** from 1600, 30 knots in an alert cruising disposition, the cruisers in column with a destroyer on either bow.
- **2143:** at the moment of ordering a reduction to 26 knots for the bombardment, three ship-like shapes were seen 15 degrees to port at about 10 km. The force "at once deployed to starboard" and battle was ordered.
- **2146:** "as the flagship was about to open fire" she received concentrated fire with flares and star shell; the first shell hit her bridge.
- **2146.5:** Furutaka, second in line, ordered action on seeing the gun flashes. Only her high-angle guns fired a salvo; her main battery "had finished training but had not yet fired".
- Kinugasa is read as firing from 2152 (one reading, not checked).

**For decision 0068:** the report supports an order to action at 2143 tied to the sighting, three minutes before the American fire, and shows neither leading cruiser had fired her main battery when it came. It names no battle stations order or numbered condition before 2143, so it does not settle what state the crews were in before the sighting. It says nothing of what the guns were loaded with, where they were trained, or any recognition signal. The two web encyclopedia rows (630 and 642) are left as they were.
