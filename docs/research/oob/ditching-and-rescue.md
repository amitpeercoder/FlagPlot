# Aircraft lost without being shot down, and what became of their crews, 1942

Status: research report for the architect, read on 2026-11-12. Nothing here is settled. The rows are in `data/oob/engagement_outcomes.csv`, ids 1370 to 1505, with 26 new gaps. Measures used: `aircraft_ditched` (the note begins with the cause and where it came down), `crews_recovered`, `crews_lost`, `aircrew_recovered`, `aircrew_lost`, `aircraft_landed_elsewhere`, `aircraft_lost_with_ship`. Nine quotations were checked a second time against the saved pages; the Japanese forms cited here were read once for this purpose.

## Disputes and gaps first

**Disputes** (each side of each has its own row)
- **Hornet's fighters at Midway.** Ten ditched for fuel. Pilots rescued: 5 by her own report and the Naval War College, 8 by the 1943 Navy narrative.
- **Enterprise's scouts at Eastern Solomons.** The Navy narrative has four forced down (three crews rescued by destroyers, one on Stewart Island). Her own report has two water landings.
- **Lexington's aircraft saved on 8 May.** 14, 16 or 18, by source.
- **Japanese ditchings at Coral Sea on 8 May.** 7 by one compilation; 5 or 6 beside Zuikaku and 2 at an island by the Naval War College.
- **Santa Cruz.** Enterprise's 14 water landings may include Hornet aircraft the Navy narrative counts separately. Not reconciled.
- **"Missing" on a Japanese unit form can include men who lived.** The Misawa air group's form for 10 September lists a bomber missing with seven dead. A website that tracks wrecks says that aircraft force-landed on New Georgia and six of the seven were captured.
- **Several US incidents over Guadalcanal are told two ways:** two or three pilots missing on 24 August; whether one pilot on 31 August parachuted into jungle or came down in the sea.

**Gaps**
- No Japanese count of ditchings at Midway, where the carriers' aircraft had no deck to return to.
- No written plane-guard doctrine for either navy, and nothing on Japanese rafts, parachutes or night pick-ups.
- Hornet's Santa Cruz report and Saratoga's reports were not found.
- The time of return against sunset is stated for three occasions only (24 August, 25 October, 4 to 5 June).
- No organised rescue flights or crash boats at Guadalcanal in 1942 were found; the first organised flying boat rescues are dated to 1943.
- No dive bomber or torpedo bomber crew fates over Guadalcanal.

## 5. Rescue arrangements, in the sources' words

- **Destroyers left behind or sent back.** Coral Sea: two destroyers "left behind on 'Search and Rescue' operations". Eastern Solomons: "The Grayson was detached at 1756 to search astern for planes which might land out of fuel in the water"; she found no one.
- **Submarines and flying boats.** Midway: three submarines were ordered "to search for survivors"; flying boats searched for rafts for days.
- **The cost of stopping.** Santa Cruz: the destroyer Porter "stopped to rescue a pilot who had landed in the water" and was torpedoed and lost. The fleet's bulletin then advised dropping a raft instead of stopping.
- **Night landings.** The same bulletin, on 25 October: "had it not been for landing crashes which tied up the deck for considerable periods, there is no doubt that several planes which landed in the water due to fuel exhaustion, would have gotten on board." Hornet at Midway: "Few of the pilots had previous night carrier landing experience."
- **The Japanese.** The Naval War College: "The Japanese, on the other hand, except on rare occasions, did not make much effort to save their pilots." Against that, Ryujo's own form has her patrol fighters picked up at once by her destroyers, and the Japanese shore-based units used Buka, Rekata Bay and Shortland as places to put a damaged aircraft down.
- **Over Guadalcanal.** Islanders and coastwatchers brought airmen back; pilots walked in through Japanese patrols; a destroyer picked one man from the water.

## What the record supports for a rule

These are my readings; the rule is the architect's.

- **Where it came down decides the crew.** Beside its own force: saved, in minutes, almost without exception. Open sea: about even, and only after days. Over the enemy's ground or far from home: lost. That holds for both navies.
- **The Japanese loss of crews over Guadalcanal was a matter of distance, not of practice.** Their fighters had nowhere to come down; their bombers lived when they reached an emergency strip.
- **Ditching for fuel went with three things:** a launch near or beyond radius; a carrier that was not where the aircraft expected it; and a return after dark, when one deck crash kept the rest in the air until their fuel ran out.
- **A lost deck did not mean lost aircraft if another deck or a field was in reach.** Most of the orphaned aircraft in the table landed somewhere; the ones lost were lost with the ship or ditched beside the escorts.
- **Stopping to pick up a crew had a price** in the presence of submarines.
