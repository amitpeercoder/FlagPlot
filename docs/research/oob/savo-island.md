# Savo Island, 8 to 9 August 1942: what the sources give a scenario

Status: research note for scenario 4c. Read on 2026-11-10. The rows behind every statement are in `data/oob/engagement_events.csv`, `engagement_positions.csv`, `engagement_outcomes.csv` and `fits.csv`, each with its page reference. This note is the reading guide. Where it and the rows differ, the rows are right.

Times are zone minus 11, the time the Allied ships kept. Ranges are as the source gives them, in yards.

## Sources, and how far to trust them

| Source | What it is | Use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bates and Innis, Naval War College, 1950 | The Navy's own analysis, built from Allied action reports, captured Japanese records and the 1945 interrogations | Almost everything below. Allied positions and actions are firm. Japanese times and ranges are Bates's reconstruction, plotted on his own diagrams |
| US Navy online ship histories and the Australian Navy's unit histories | Current official short histories, read in a browser | Chicago's radar, Bagley's part, Canberra's losses. Thin on equipment |
| ONI Combat Narrative, 1943 | Wartime account from Allied reports | Allied readiness and what each ship saw. It says its own times are approximate. Poor on the Japanese |
| BuShips War Damage Report 29, 1943 | Damage to Quincy, Astoria and Vincennes from survivors' statements | Hits and sinking times for those three |
| combinedfleet.com ship records | Modern compilation from Japanese records | Japanese ammunition and damage. Half its pages would not load |

Bates was read as a scan through machine-read text, in which digits are sometimes misread. **Printed pages 106 to 108, 110 to 112 and 114 to 117 have since been checked against the page images of the scan.** They cover the Japanese sightings in the first two encounters and the Allied positions at 0000 and 0132. Most figures were as first read. These were corrected from the images:

- Aoba and Kako sighted Jarvis at 0135, not 0136.
- Furutaka took bearings on Blue at 0121 and 0126, not 0116.
- Chokai turned to course 150 at 0105, not 160.
- Patterson was 5,500 yards from Chokai at 0140, not 6,500.
- Ralph Talbot's nearest approach to the column was 13,000 yards, not 15,000.
- Australia left at 2123, not 2125.
- At 0132 the southern group bore 146 degrees from Savo, not 140, and Blue 273 degrees, not 275. At 0000 Ralph Talbot bore 013 degrees, not 015.

One conflict is confirmed as printed and stands: the range at which Chokai sighted Blue is 10,900 yards on p.107 and 8,700 yards on p.110. Rows checked this way say so. The other Bates rows, about 430, have not been checked against images, and page numbers in their locators can be one out. The plates are bound into the same scan and are legible, but were not used for positions.

Not read at all: Gill's Australian official history, the Japanese interrogations themselves (only as Bates quotes them), Morison, Loxton, Frank.

## The detection sequence

This is the question the scenario turns on, and the sources agree on its shape: **the Japanese saw every Allied group first, by eye, at ranges two to four times those at which any Allied ship saw them. No Allied radar detected a Japanese ship before the shooting started.**

Mikawa altered course, held fire and passed between the two pickets, which were about 14 miles apart at the time. Bates blames land echoes, tired operators and a set better at aircraft than ships. ONI calls the failure unexplained.

### What the sources do not say

- Any radar range at which Blue or Ralph Talbot could have held the column. Bates gives only the SC set's "reliable range of four to ten miles" and says land spoiled it.
- Whether Chicago's or Canberra's search radar was switched on. A modern Australian article says Canberra's surface set was working and masked by land. It gives no references.
- Why Chokai could see Vincennes at 18,000 yards. Bates offers fires at Tulagi, lightning or a floatplane report.

The two cruiser groups could be as much as 17 miles apart depending on where each was in its patrol.

Mikawa's track, in brief: all eight ships were based at Kavieng and Rabaul. He left Rabaul in Chokai at 1628 on 7 August, was passed by the submarine S-38 off Cape St George at about 1946, rounded Buka at 0100 on the 8th, and spent the morning east of Bougainville launching and recovering floatplanes, where two Hudsons found him at 1026 and 1101. He entered the Slot at 1600 at 24 knots. At 0000 on the 9th he was 37 miles from Cape Esperance at 26 knots. He turned north-east past Savo at 0132, three miles off. At 0144 the column split, Chokai and four cruisers passing east of the northern group, Tenryu, Yubari and Furutaka west of it. He ordered withdrawal at 0220 and went up the Slot at 30 knots. `engagement_positions.csv` has the track points with times.

What they had been told:

- The Hudson sighting of 1026 reached Turner and Crutchley at about 1645, or about 1900 in another place in Bates, most of a day's light gone, and garbled into "seaplane tenders".
- Turner and Crutchley concluded the force was going to set up a seaplane base at Rekata Bay and would not attack that night. Riefkohl wrote the possibility of a cruiser attack into his own night orders and told no other ship.
- Crutchley's orders to the pickets were to report and shadow. They said nothing about what the cruisers should do if the enemy arrived unannounced.
- Ralph Talbot's 2345 aircraft warning reached Crutchley at about 0145.

## What the class data can and cannot give the combat rule

The rule asks for guns, rate of fire, shell weight, torpedo tubes, torpedo and warhead, a measure of how much damage a ship can take, and radar on the night.

| Input | State |
| --- | --- |
| Number of main guns | Sourced for all eleven classes. Quincy and Vincennes carried the Mark 12 gun and Astoria an earlier mark; the class row says Mark 9. For Furutaka and Kako the bore is 20.32 cm on every reading, but whether the guns were the "No. 2" pattern or re-bored "No. 1" guns is disputed, and one website says both in different footnotes |
| Rounds carried per main gun | **New.** Japanese 8-inch cruisers 120 (a figure for the gun, not the class). Canberra's class 130. Chicago's class about 167 early in the war (1,500 a ship). New Orleans class about 156 (1,400 a ship). Bagley and Benham classes 300. All from one weapons reference site. **Nothing found for the 14 cm guns of Tenryu and Yubari or the 12 cm guns of Yunagi** |
| Rate of fire | Still one website for the designed rate of every gun, and its figures for the Japanese 8-inch range from 3 to 5 depending on which sentence is read. **New:** a separate attribute for the rate crews achieved, where a source says so: about 2 for the older Japanese 8-inch mounting, 3 to 4 sustained for the British 8-inch. And from the night itself: the Japanese heavy cruisers averaged 1.2 to 1.6 rounds per gun per minute over their firing window, Astoria 0.25 |

## What a rule would need to model

- **Visual detection at night with a large gap between the sides.** Japanese first sightings at 8,700 to 18,000 yards against Allied ones at 3,000 to 5,000 on the same night, in the same weather. Bates credits optics, training and rest. The sources give no way to separate the three.
- **Radar that fails near land.** Two working search sets missed eight ships passing within ten miles. Fire-control radar then gave good ranges once the target was lit.
- **A warning that does not arrive.** Three reports reached someone who could have acted (the Hudson sighting, the 2345 aircraft, Patterson's broadcast) and each was late, garbled or not passed on. A side's alert state at first contact is a consequence of that, not a starting constant.
- **Readiness as a state with a cost.** Condition II after two days awake: guns half manned and captains asleep. No source read gives one figure for the time from alarm to first salvo. Bates says setting Condition I from Condition II took about five minutes in normal practice, a minute or so more at night (outcomes 3917, 3925), and "possibly ten minutes" in Quincy (outcome 3924), whose first salvo he puts about eight to twelve minutes after general quarters (outcomes 3059, 3028). The times printed for each ship's alarm and first fire are in the events (Astoria 90, 94, 96; Vincennes 97, 1063 to 1065; Canberra 59, 79; Quincy 1048, 1066) and in outcomes 3859 to 3941. An earlier wording here, "two to five minutes", had no row behind it and was removed on 13 November 2026.
- **Surprise that is worth minutes.** The attacker had torpedoes running for five minutes before detection and the first shells landed within a minute or two of it.
- **Illumination.** Aircraft flares to silhouette, then searchlights at under 10,000 yards. Allied star shell mostly failed.
- **Command gaps.** A group whose commander has left and whose stand-in gives no orders fights as single ships.
