# Fuelling at sea, December 1941 to 1945

Status: a research report for the architect, read on 2026-11-21, for decision 0201 (refuelling at sea, planned). Facts and their strength only: nothing here is settled, and nothing proposes a rule or a game figure. Rows: `ship_facts.csv` 12834 to 12923 (89 `fuelling_note` rows and one `movement_note`), `sources.csv` 14041 to 14046, and eight lines at the end of `gaps.csv`. The earlier notes on the same subject are `fuel-on-station.md` (sections 2d, 2f, 5) and `fuelling-in-port.md`; their rows are not repeated.

## How it was read

- Every page was saved whole (outside the repository) and a text copy made from it. Every quotation in a row, and every quotation in a row's note, was checked by program as a contiguous run of that text, with runs of whitespace collapsed. Archive.org texts are machine-read scans: their quotations keep the scan's errors ("oo" for "c", line-break hyphens), as the rows say.
- Three researchers under me read the US action reports and analyses, the Japanese oilers' records and the 1943 to 1945 record; I read the Pacific Fleet's fuelling instructions of 1942, Wildenberg and Carter myself and checked every quotation used here or in a row against the saved page. Their summaries were leads; nothing is filed on a summary.
- Japanese times are as the combinedfleet records print them. Where those records name a clock it is Tokyo time (JST), which in the Solomons and the Central Pacific runs about 1.5 to 2 hours behind local time; most entries name none. An entry that runs past about 1830 or begins before 0600 is called a night entry below on the clock as printed; at 160E local time is later still.
- Rates in tons or barrels an hour are my arithmetic on the quantities and hours printed (barrels to long tons: 0.1516, `fuel-on-station.md` section 3).

## Answers to the seven questions

### 1. Under way or stopped

**United States: under way, alongside, in every case of record, from before the war.** The 1942 bulletin says "Fueling at sea is accomplished while the heavier ship tows the other alongside", destroyers on both sides of an oiler at once if weather permits, large ships normally on the oiler's port side only. "The standard speed for the ship which is being approached is 8 knots"; once rigged, speed rises by half knots; 8 knots is the most in heavy weather; the most an oiler can make while pumping is 12 knots for the Cimarron class, 10 for the Brazos and 8 for the Sepulga. Course: the wind or sea about two points on the unengaged bow, directly into the weather when both sides are used, almost any heading in mild weather with practised ships; zigzagging "should not be undertaken when fueling battleships, carriers or cruisers" unless necessary, and changes of course in steps of not more than 10 degrees (paragraphs 105, 111 to 116, 1942). Strength: high, a primary instruction of the Pacific Fleet.

- How that came to be (Wildenberg, both works): before 1917 fuelling between ships was done "only in protected waters with the vessels moored together"; Maumee fuelled destroyers under way, riding abeam, in mid-Atlantic in May 1917; the US Navy fuelled destroyers abeam under way through the 1920s and 1930s (Cuyama at 4 knots in 1925, Salinas at 3.5 knots in 1931); the astern method, tried by Kanawha from 1924, gave about 15,000 to 16,000 gallons an hour at about 6 knots and was given up by 1931; the first broadside fuelling of a carrier was Kanawha to Saratoga at 7 knots on 13 June 1939. So "stopped" was the practice of before 1917, not of 1941.
- In the war: 8 to 12 knots as "standard fueling speed" in the Gilberts in 1943 (Morison VII; Wildenberg); 10 to 12 knots for general fuellings and destroyers topped off at up to 17 knots in September 1944 (Carter ch. 17); 10 knots on course 110 for Task Group 38.1 on 4 June 1945 (its report); "normally into the wind at about 10 knots speed" in 1945 (Carter ch. 28); 16 knots for destroyers from battleships on 21 January 1945 (Morison XIII); 4.3 knots for tugs with a tow in October 1944 (Morison XII).
- From 1943 the receiving ship made the approach on the oiler and the breast and spring lines went (Wildenberg ch. 17, the Gilberts); the November 1944 COMINCH instructions still prescribed the towline as "an exercise in position keeping" (Wildenberg ch. 18); the astern method was by then "an emergency measure only" except for diesel oil (same).
- Stopped or at anchor in open sea: none found, 1941 to 1945. A battleship "lying to for fueling" a destroyer in Leyte Gulf on 30 November 1944 (Sumner's war diary) is inside a gulf; Lexington fuelled at anchor in Lahaina Roads in 1937. Not found is not the same as never: see "What stays unknown".

**Japan: under way where a record says, method mixed.** The oilers were fitted before the Hawaii operation "for refueling undertow" and "for simultaneous port and starboard refueling" (Kyokuto Maru, 30 October and 10 November 1941); Shinkoku Maru refuelled destroyers "sideways" on 24 January 1942 and "while sailing" on 31 March and 3 April 1942; Tsurumi began "towing refueling" on 23 February 1942; Nichiei Maru fuelled the damaged Mogami "using the stern-to-bow technique" on 7 June 1942; from October 1942 the records put ships on an oiler's starboard and port sides at once (Genyo Maru 28 October, Shinkoku Maru 13 November, Nichiei Maru 16 November, "with hoses"). Toho Maru fuelled the carriers "At slow speed" on 6 May 1942; Kyokuto Maru fuelled destroyers "while steaming at 14 knots" on 21 October 1942, the only Japanese fuelling speed found. "towing refueling" was still exercised in May 1944 (Azusa Maru). No source read names a Japanese doctrine or the date of a change of method (gap). Strength: medium; a modern compilation of Japanese records, with speeds almost never given.

### 2. Night

**Both navies fuelled at night when an operation needed it; the US Navy in 1942 called it new, and by 1945 had a rule against it.**

- United States, dated: 28 January 1942, Enterprise alongside Platte after dark for five and a half hours before the Marshalls raid; Wildenberg says no heavy ship had been fuelled in the open sea at night before (row 12897). Night of 2 February 1942, the destroyers again (Carter p. 17). 3 May 1942, Task Force 11 ordered to fuel its destroyers "on a north- westerly course at night" to keep the next morning's rendezvous; Bates calls it "a sound one" (row 12907). 8 June 1942, Enterprise finished fuelling from Cimarron at 2200 local, after twilight quarters at 2051 (row 12916; Bates has 2000). 7 August 1942, Gwin fuelled by North Carolina "during darkness, which required a new skill for the 1942 United States Navy" (Dyer, row 12917). 21 July 1943, the Kiska force fuelled from Guadalupe at night in thick fog (Morison VII, row 12920). 7 November 1944, three carrier groups fuelled from 0930 until 2130 (Carter ch. 21). 22 June 1944, fuelling "until nightfall" and finished next day (Morison VIII): stopped by dark.
- United States, rules: the 1942 bulletin says nothing of night (its whole text was searched). Halsey's lessons of late 1944 say nothing of it. The only rule found is of 1945: in the Okinawa logistics group "Fueling at sea was only undertaken during daylight hours due to the hazards of night maneuvering, especially under the blackout conditions required in a combat area" (Wildenberg ch. 18); groups formed at night and began "as soon as there was sufficient light" (Carter ch. 28; Morison XIV), and at the Hagushi anchorage "All fueling was done during daylight, as the harbor was completely blacked out at night" (Carter ch. 27).
- Japan, dated: 6 December 1941 about 2100, Abukuma and Tanikaze fuelled from the oilers; 8 December 1941 from 0230, Akigumo 250 t (row 12836); 5 May 1942, the Coral Sea striking force fuelled from about 2000, done before 0600 (Bates), and the night of 6 to 7 May was broken off "In the predawn hours" (Toho Maru); 15 September 1942, Myoko 1710 to 2135; 17 September, Kirishima 1720 to 2140; 12 to 13 October, two oilers from 1300 to 0330; 17 to 18 October, Chikuma 2050 to 0317 and Suzuya 2017 to 0600; 18 to 19 October, Haruna 2000 to 0020; 20 October, Maya and Myoko to 2000 and 2050; 21 October to 2200 at 14 knots; 25 October, Atago 2318 to 2331; 28 October from 0400; 16 November, five destroyers 0800 to 2330; 17 November, Atago at 0209; 16 to 17 June 1944, Takao 2310 to 0150. In October and November 1942 night fuelling was routine in the Japanese supply force. No Japanese rule on night fuelling was found.

Ships at once and in a day:

- United States: destroyers both sides at once, large ships port side only (bulletin 1942); "Cimarron efficiently fueled two destroyers simultaneously under adverse weather conditions" (Hornet report, 12 April 1942); Halsey, late 1944: "Two large ships may be fueled simultaneously from one oiler under favorable conditions with some sacrifice of pumping rate", two battleships from one tanker only in emergency for fear of crushing her (Wildenberg ch. 18; Carter ch. 17); by 1945 two at once was usual, older tankers one large ship at a time (Wildenberg ch. 18); "All tankers were equipped to fuel on both sides simultaneously" (Carter ch. 23, Okinawa). In a day: Cimarron a carrier and eight destroyers on 3 August 1942 and four more on the 4th (Bates, Savo; row 1831); Platte a cruiser and two destroyers in 3 h 30 min, 6 June 1942 (rows 12912 to 12914); Task Force 16 from two oilers 0545 to 1445 on 9 June 1942, all but Atlanta (Bates); September 1944 general fuellings of a carrier group took 6 h 11 min to 7 h 41 min (Carter ch. 17).
- Japan: fitted in November 1941 for one ship each side; in October and November 1942 one each side (Genyo Maru 28 October), or two on one side (Shinkoku Maru 13 November: Nagara starboard, Harusame and Sendai port), five destroyers in a day both sides (Nichiei Maru 16 November); one oiler with two cruisers through a night (Toho Maru 17 to 18 October). By 1943 Toho Maru had "refueled over 400 ships and transfered over 110,000-tons of fuel since the Hawaiian Operation" (her record, note of 29 March 1943).

### 4. The threat

- Doctrine, US 1942: fuelling at sea "is a military operation" in a theatre "exposed to belligerent operations", to be done "in the least possible time"; "Even during drill it should be assumed that attack is imminent"; gear to be cast off "instantly when so ordered" (bulletin paras 101 and 102; repeated in the COMINCH instructions of November 1944, Wildenberg ch. 18). No time to break away is stated anywhere read (gap).
- Practice: Task Force 17 broke off at once on a radar contact on 6 May 1942, took an anti-aircraft disposition and resumed (row 12909); it kept fuelling after a submarine was seen 15 miles from Task Force 11 on 2 May (Bates p. 29), and Bates's lesson was to change fuelling areas "frequently and radically from day to day" after 50 hours of fuelling in an area about 45 by 60 miles; Midway, 8 June 1942: the cruisers flew a six-plane inner air patrol over the fuelling ships (row 12916); from 1943 fuelling rendezvous were changed daily and radio kept down "to reduce the possibility of submarine attack" (Wildenberg ch. 17; Carter ch. 10); in 1944 fuelling areas were set "at the extreme limit of enemy land-based air" and escort carriers gave the oiler groups air and anti-submarine cover (Morison XII); Halsey: "our oiler groups for STALEMATE were pure submarine bait", one escort for each oiler (Wildenberg ch. 18).
- Japan: Hara persuaded Takagi to break off refuelling before dawn on 7 May 1942 to search; Cruiser Division 6 broke off its fuelling from Iro "immediately" on 8 May, not knowing where the enemy was (Bates p. 109); escorts named (Inazuma, Suzukaze, Shigure and Yugure in October and November 1942). No doctrine found.
- Ships caught: **Neosho and Sims were not fuelling when struck on 7 May 1942.** Detached at 1725 on 6 May, they were on passage to the oilers' rendezvous (Bates p. 64, row 12910; Task Force 17 report; DANFS). **Neches was sunk on 23 January 1942**, alone on passage to meet a destroyer for the Wake raid, not in December 1941 (Graybook). Caught while fuelling: Saranac, Neshanic and Saugatuck, fuelling four destroyers and escorts at 10 knots 40 miles south-east of Saipan, were bombed at 1630 on 18 June 1944; Saranac was left dead in the water (Morison VIII; H-Gram 032). Destroyers fuelling from North Carolina and Washington at 16 knots on 21 January 1945 were in the force struck by kamikazes (Morison XIII). Japanese: none found hit while fuelling at sea; the supply force of the Philippine Sea had finished fuelling at 0520 on 20 June 1944 and was bombed at 1746 (Azusa Maru). Mississinewa was sunk at anchor at Ulithi (Morison XII), not at sea.

### 5. Weather

- Wake relief, 22 to 23 December 1941: four destroyers in ten hours, towlines parted, seven hoses burst; the force 425 miles from Wake with four destroyers still to fuel when recalled (Wildenberg ch. 17). Neches could make no more than 12 3/4 knots.
- Hawaii operation, 3 or 4 December 1941: ships rolled up to 45 degrees and refuelling was cancelled (the oilers' records disagree on the day); the final refuelling on 6 December ran 0630 to 0810.
- Tokyo raid: 9 April 1942 too rough, Hornet's fuelling deferred, two of Cimarron's men lost overboard; 17 April the heavy ships' fuelling "barely completed" before a gale (rows 12901, 12906).
- 1944 to 1945: 8 October 1944, Platte gave 48,000 bbl in confused swells and lost three sections of hose (row 12923); Typhoon Cobra, 17 to 18 December 1944, fuelling begun, belayed at 1251 and stopped at 1310, tried again and given up on the 18th, with Spence at 15 per cent and Hickox at 14 per cent of capacity (Morison XIII, Nimitz 14CL-45); 17 January 1945 only partly fuelled, the oilers kept company overnight at 8 knots (Morison XIII); 4 June 1945 fuelling stopped at 0936 for a storm and finished from 1700 to 1930 (Task Group 38.1).

### 6. Speed of advance and distance from the enemy

- Speed made good: Enterprise 10.4 knots noon to noon on the fuelling day of 8 June 1942 and 6 knots on 9 June (war diary, row 12916); the Coral Sea forces fuelled for about 50 hours within an area about 45 by 60 miles (Bates p. 30); the Wake relief's advance was slowed by Neches's speed and two days fuelling destroyers (Wildenberg). After fuelling the Tokyo raid's carriers went on at 20 to 25 knots without destroyers and oilers (row 12906; Hornet report).
- Distance: Fletcher meant to fuel just outside the enemy's air search from Wake (Wildenberg); in August 1942 he withdrew about 500 miles south of the unloading area to fuel (`fuel-on-station.md` section 4); 1944: fuelling areas at the edge of enemy land-based air (Morison XII), about 450 miles east of Manila on 6 November 1944, and 220 miles east of the point of retirement after the Philippine Sea; Typhoon Cobra's rendezvous was chosen as "the nearest spot to Luzon outside Japanese fighter-plane radius" (Morison XIII); 1945, Task Group 50.8's station was about three days from Ulithi and less than two from Okinawa (Morison XIV).

### 7. Warships fuelling their own destroyers

- Common in the US Navy from 1942. Fletcher's practice at the Coral Sea was to top off destroyers "from tankers, cruisers, or carrier whenever they could receive as much as five hundred barrels" (Task Force 17 report), since it was much quicker than from the few oilers (Bates p. 29). Dated: the heavy cruisers Indianapolis and Pensacola, 12 March 1942 (Carter); Hornet, 11 April 1942 (row 12904); Portland to Task Force 17's destroyers 1230 to 1800, 5 June 1942 (row 12911); North Carolina to Gwin at night, 7 August 1942 (row 12917); North Carolina to her screen 0750 to 1205, 27 October 1944 (row 12921); North Carolina and Washington at 16 knots, 21 January 1945. Against it: Fletcher in 1942, cruisers had fuel "for 50 hours at 25 knots" and could not spare it (Dyer p. 393); in 1944 Wichita burned too fast to top off destroyers (Carter ch. 17). Halsey's rule of 1944: destroyers "must be fueled by heavy ships waiting their turn" (Wildenberg ch. 18); in September 1944 Task Group 38.1's large ships topped off 53 destroyers between tanker fuellings (Carter ch. 17). The 1942 bulletin says its principles apply "if a battleship or an auxiliary is to deliver the oil".
- Japan: "Japanese policy was to refuel all ships at every opportunity", battleships used to fuel small ships (Bates, Midway p. 21); dated: the fleets refuel destroyers at sea, 15 to 17 September 1942 (rows 5354 and after); Kongo and Haruna refuel the destroyers, 14 November 1942 (Kirishima's record); cruisers fuelled destroyers at Coron in October 1944 for want of a tanker (USSBS interrogation 58). No rate found for either navy beyond the hours above.

## Whether the designer's understanding holds

- **"Fuelling at night did happen when operations and schedules required it": holds in full, for both navies.** United States: January 1942 (Enterprise, the first heavy ship fuelled at sea at night by Wildenberg's account), May, June and August 1942, July 1943, November 1944; Dyer calls it a new skill in 1942; the US Navy's own 1942 instruction neither allows nor forbids it, and a daylight-only rule is found only for the logistics groups of 1945. Japan: routine in the supply force from October 1942, and done from December 1941. Strength: high for the US events (primary reports and two histories agree), medium for the Japanese (one compilation, clock not always named).
- **"Ships at first stopped or lay nearly still to fuel, but the submarine threat forced them to keep under way": does not hold for the war.** The US Navy fuelled under way from 1917 for destroyers and from 1939 for carriers; every fuelling of 1941 to 1945 found was under way, at a standard 8 knots in 1942 and 8 to 12 knots later; stopping in open sea is not found in any year of the war. The submarine danger appears in the sources as a reason to fuel fast, to keep gear ready to cast off and to move fuelling areas and screen the oilers, and (in Wildenberg's view, not a 1942 statement) as a reason the slow astern method was not used, not as a cause of a change from stopped to moving. The part that does hold: fuelling was slow steaming, 8 knots against cruising speeds of 15 to 25, and on a fuelling day a force made good 6 to 10 knots. Before 1917, ships fuelled moored together in protected waters. For Japan, no case of ships stopped at sea was found either; the records say "while sailing", "at slow speed" and "while steaming at 14 knots" where they say anything, and most entries do not say.
- The plan's two assumptions, "stopped" and "daylight only", are both contrary to the record of 1942 for both navies; "daylight only" matches the US practice of 1945.

## Where sources disagree

- **Enterprise, 8 June 1942:** Bates has her fuelling from 1400 to 2000; her war diary has the force's fuelling begin at 0654 and hers completed at 2200 local. Rows 12915 and 12916 are `disputed`.
- **The night fuelling of Enterprise:** `fuelling-in-port.md` (no row) dated it to the Tokyo raid in April 1942 from a photograph caption; Wildenberg's text puts it on 28 January 1942 with Platte, and Carter has Platte topping off the Enterprise group that day. Wildenberg's sentence naming "the entire Lexington group" is read as a slip (row 12897's note).
- **Hornet, 12 April 1942:** Carter, "the Hornet supplied 400,000 gallons"; Hornet's report, Cimarron "fueled Hornet". Carter's row is `unconfirmed`.
- **Kongo, 19 October 1942:** 1,270 t from Toei Maru at 0800 (her record) and 840 t from Kyokuto Maru (his record); whether one transfer or two is not said. Both rows `disputed`.
- **Atago, 21 November 1942:** the entry prints "(29th)"; date of the end uncertain; `unconfirmed`.
- **Japanese rates of June 1944** (Yamato, Atago, Kumano) are ten times those of 1942; filed `low`, `unconfirmed`.
- **The Hawaii operation's cancelled fuelling:** 3 December (Kenyo Maru) or 4 December 1941 (Kyokuto Maru).
- **Bates on the Coral Sea, Task Force 11's start:** about 0800 on 1 May on p. 28 and "early on 2 May" in the next passage; the Task Force 17 report has Task Force 11 not expecting to finish before noon on 4 May.
- **Two leads of the brief were wrong:** Neosho and Sims were not fuelling when attacked (rows 12910, 3388); Neches was sunk in January 1942, not during the Wake relief.

## Statements for which no attribute exists

These are held here, with their sources, and not as rows. Each would need an attribute the architect has not made:

1. **A navy's doctrine for fuelling at sea** (speed, course, zigzag, sides, design rate a hose, whether at night): the 1942 bulletin's figures above; the 1945 daylight rule. `training_facts.csv` is by navy but its attribute list is closed in `oob-check`; no other file holds a navy's statement.
2. **An oiler class's figures for fuelling at sea:** the most it can make while pumping (Cimarron class 12 knots, Brazos 10, Sepulga 8, Halawa and Kaloli 7, bulletin 1942); hoses and sides; "Large ships will normally be fueled only on the port side". No ship_class attribute holds these.
3. **Cargo fuel oil in barrels** (not bunkers): Wildenberg's table 19, the Gilberts fuelling group of November 1943, gives cargo oil of Cimarron 103,233 bbl, Platte 101,638, Sabine 104,938, Guadalupe 104,938; the Graybook (about May 1942) "SAKINE and CIMARRON can carry 105,000 bbls each". `fuel_capacity_note` is for a ship's own fuel and `cargo_capacity_lt` is in tons, unconverted; neither fits. Stage B of decision 0201 needs this.
4. **A fuelling at sea as an event between two ships.** `fuelling_note` is about one ship; where the receiving ships are unnamed the row is filed on the giving ship and its note says so (rows 12907 to 12910 and 12919 to 12923, and row 12871 for Kyokuto Maru on 21 October 1942). An attribute naming giver and receiver would remove that strain.

## What stays unknown

- The Japanese method as doctrine and when it changed; Japanese fuelling speeds other than 14 knots once; Japanese measures against submarines while fuelling (gaps).
- A pump log: barrels or tons an hour through one hose in a dated 1942 US fuelling; hours alongside with quantity for a US heavy ship in 1942 other than Enterprise on 8 June (gaps).
- A US rule on night fuelling before 1945; the February 1942 bulletin; the COMINCH instructions of November 1944 (gap).
- How long it took to cast off and break away under attack: required "instantly", never timed in anything read.
- The primary record of the Wake relief fuelling, and the reports of Neosho's captain and of Sims's loss (gaps; the scans could not be read).
- Who fuelled whom in many Japanese entries, and the clock their times are in.
